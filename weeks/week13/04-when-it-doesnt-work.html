When It Doesn’t Work

Sections 13.13 – 13.16  |  Estimated time: ~45 minutes

13.13   Why everything can’t happen at once

Your CPU takes six transfers to do something as small as ADD R1, R2. A natural question is why the control unit doesn’t just do it all in one go. The answer is the one fact this whole design is built round: the bus carries one value at a time.

  • Fetch has three values to move: an address to the MAR, an instruction to the IR, a new address to the PC. Each needs the bus to itself, and each depends on the one before — the RAM cannot put out the right byte until the MAR holds the address, and the PC cannot take PC + 1 until ACC has it.
  • An ALU instruction has three more: RB to TMP, RA through the ALU to ACC, ACC to RB. The ALU needs two inputs and the bus supplies one, so TMP has to be loaded a step early.
  • Inside each transfer, timing matters too. The source must be driving before the destination opens, and must keep driving until it closes: enable on, set on, set off, enable off (Week 11). The stepper only moves to the next step in the last quarter, when every enable is off, so no two steps’ drivers ever overlap.

So the six steps are not slowness for its own sake. They are the minimum number of one-value transfers that the work needs on a single bus. Week 15 puts a number on what it costs, and on what real processors spend to avoid it.

13.14   Systematic signal tracing

Your Checkpoint 6 CPU has about a dozen tabs and hundreds of wires. When R2 ends up holding the wrong value, staring at the whole circuit will not find the cause. Tracing will. The idea is to start from the wrong value and walk backwards, one question at a time, until you reach a wire that is not what the control table says it should be.

  1. Find the first wrong value. Run one instruction (24 clicks) at a time and compare every register with a trace you trust — page 3’s Program Runner gives you one for any program. The first instruction after which something differs is where to look; everything after it is a consequence.
  2. Find the step that wrote it. Every part is written by one set line, and the control table says at which steps: RB at step 6, ACC at steps 1 and 5, the PC at step 3, the IR at step 2. Click to that step’s set quarter.
  3. Check the lines. At that quarter, is the right set line high, and is every other set line low? Is the right enable high, and only one? Put probes on the control unit’s outputs if you have not.
  4. Check the bus. Is it the value that should be captured? If it shows E, two parts are driving it, or the one driving it holds E. If it floats, nothing is driving it.
  5. Check the driver. The part that should be driving: is its enable high, and does its reg probe show the right value? If its value is wrong, it was written wrongly earlier — go back to step 2 for that part.
Seeing inside a part in Logisim

The MAR and the 32 RAM bytes have no pins on your CPU tab. To see them while the circuit runs, right-click the RAM_16X16 instance and choose View RAM_16X16: Logisim opens the subcircuit showing this instance’s live values, and each RAM_BYTE’s mem pin shows its byte. To get back, open Simulate → Go Out To State: it lists every circuit above the one you are viewing, so choose CPU to return to your CPU tab with its live values. If you went in more than one level, you can jump straight back to any of them. The same works for CONTROL_UNIT when you want to see its gates.

13.16   Where you are

Your CPU runs programs. It cannot yet make a decision: every program runs straight through, byte 0 to the end, and it cannot read or write data in memory. Week 14 adds the rest of the instruction set — LD, ST and DATA, the jumps that give the PC a value other than PC + 1, carry-in, and END to stop the clock — and a loader so that a program arrives from a file instead of your fingers. Then your CPU runs MYSIM.txt, and multiplies.
