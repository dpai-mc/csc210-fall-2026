Naming a Register

Sections 10.9 – 10.12  |  Estimated time: ~45 minutes

10.9   What a register number is

On page 2 you ran ADD R0, R1 by raising R1’s e, then R0’s, then pulsing R1’s s. Every register has its own pair of lines, eight lines in all for R0–R3, and choosing a register meant choosing which line to touch.

An instruction cannot touch a line. It can only carry bits. Four registers need two bits to name one of them — 00 for R0 through 11 for R3 — and something has to turn those two bits into one line out of four. You built that something in Week 3: DECODER_2TO4, whose 2-bit sel raises exactly one of out0–out3. A register number is a decoder input. That is all “R2” means to the hardware.

10.10   REG_SELECT

An ALU operation names two registers, RA and RB, and uses them three ways: RA drives the bus, RB drives the bus, and RB captures the result. So there are three decoders, each followed by four AND gates that let its chosen line through only when one control line is high:

DecoderIts selANDed withProduces
Ararega_e“RA, drive the bus”
Brbregb_e“RB, drive the bus”
B′rbregb_s“RB, capture” — these four are r0_s–r3_s

Each register’s enable can be asked for by either of the first two decoders, so r0_e is decoder A’s line 0 OR decoder B’s line 0, and likewise for the other three. The whole circuit is three decoders, twelve AND gates and four OR gates.

Why that OR is safe — a third kind of guarantee

If rega_e and regb_e were ever high together, with RA and RB different, two registers would drive the bus at once. Nothing in REG_SELECT’s wiring prevents it. What prevents it is timing: the control unit raises regb_e in one step and rega_e in the next, never both.

You have now seen all three ways a design keeps one driver on a wire. The ALU’s output stage guarantees it by structure — its decoder can only select one leg. The bus guarantees nothing by itself and pays for controlled buffers so parts can let go. REG_SELECT relies on the sequence the control unit follows. The third is the cheapest and the most fragile: it is correct only as long as the control unit is.

When ra and rb name the same register — XOR R3, R3 — nothing special happens. R3 drives the bus in one transfer because regb_e asked, and again in the next because rega_e asked; the OR does not care which.

10.11   Four registers plus REG_SELECT is a register file

A register file is a set of registers that are reached by number rather than by name. R0–R3 and REG_SELECT are exactly that, and now you can set yours beside the MIPS register file you programmed in Weeks 5–8 and watched in Week 9:

MIPSYour CPU
Registers an instruction can name32, $zero–$ra4, R0–R3
Bits per register field52
Registers read at oncetwo, in one cycleone per transfer — the bus carries one value
Where the second operand waitsnowhere; both arrive togetherTMP
Register fields in an ALU instructionthree: rs, rt, rdtwo: RA, RB — RB is both a source and the destination

None of those differences is a different idea. MIPS spends hardware to do in one cycle what your CPU does in three transfers, and spends instruction bits on a third register field so add $t0, $t1, $t2 can leave both sources intact. Your ADD R1, R2 overwrites R2 — a cost you pay in instructions, the way MIPS pays in wires.

10.12   Who drives these lines

Look back: the orientation video, 21:08 to 21:31

Your instructor’s registers run R0 on the right to R3 on the left. Any order works, as long as REG_SELECT’s r0_e and r0_s go to the register you call R0, and so on — the number is decided by the wire, not by where the box sits.

Today, you do: in Checkpoint 4, ra, rb, rega_e, regb_e and regb_s are input pins you click. Next week the control unit takes over. The two register fields will come straight from the bits of the instruction being executed, and the three control lines from the stepper, one step at a time.
