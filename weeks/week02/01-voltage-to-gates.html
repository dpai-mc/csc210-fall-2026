From Voltage to Decision

Estimated time: ~1.75 hours (includes an 8-minute video)  |  Sections: 2.1–2.2  |  Prerequisite: Week 2 Orientation

What We Have / What We Can't Do / What We Can Now Do

What you already know: Data is stored as patterns of 0s and 1s, where 0 means no voltage and 1 means voltage present.

What you can't do yet: Explain what sits between "voltage on a wire" and "the machine decided something." Right now that is a black box.

What you will be able to do by the end of this page: Describe the transistor as a voltage-controlled switch, explain why a single transistor behaves as a NOT gate, read and write truth tables for all seven logic gates with any number of inputs, and recognize their circuit symbols on sight.

Last week you learned that any data can be represented as 0s and 1s. You converted between binary, hexadecimal, and decimal. You saw that the same byte pattern can mean an integer, a character, or a color — depending on how the CPU interprets it.

But representation is passive. The bits just sit there.

How does the computer actually do anything with those bits? How does it add two numbers, compare values, or decide whether to execute one instruction or another? How does voltage — which is either present or absent — become a decision?

The answer is the logic gate. A logic gate is a tiny circuit that takes one or more binary inputs and produces a binary output according to a fixed rule. These rules seem trivial, but they are the atoms of computation. Every operation your computer performs — from rendering this page to running a compiler — is the result of billions of logic gates making billions of tiny decisions per second.

2.1 — From Voltage to Decision: Transistors as Switches

Last week we said that 0 means "no voltage" and 1 means "voltage present." But how does voltage become a decision? The bridge is the transistor.

The Transistor as a Switch

A transistor is a tiny electronic switch. In its simplest role in digital logic, it has three terminals:

  • Gate — the control input. Voltage here determines whether the switch is open or closed. This is the voltage in of the video.
  • Source — where current enters, from the 5-volt supply.
  • Drain — where current exits, toward ground.
Two names for the same thing

The video says "voltage in" and "voltage out"; the schematic below says "gate" and labels a separate output node. They describe the same circuit. Gate = voltage in. The output is not a terminal of the transistor at all — it is a point on the wire, which is exactly why the next few paragraphs matter.

A transistor used as a switch. Voltage on the gate decides whether current may pass from source to drain.

When voltage is applied to the gate, the transistor conducts — current flows from source to drain, and the switch is closed. When no voltage is applied, the transistor blocks — no current flows, and the switch is open. That is the whole behavior of the device.

This is the entire physical mechanism. A transistor converts a voltage signal into a decision: let current through, or don't. Modern CPUs contain billions of transistors, each making this binary decision billions of times per second.

But Where Is the Output?

Notice what the paragraph above did not say. It said whether current flows. It did not say what the output is — because a transistor by itself has no output. To read a 1 or a 0 you have to build a small circuit around it and measure the voltage at a particular point on a wire. Which point you pick determines the answer.

Here is the circuit the video draws. The 5-volt supply reaches the output node through a resistor, and the transistor sits between that node and ground:

One transistor, one resistor. The output is the wire between them — and it reads the opposite of the input.

Now trace it, and watch the output rather than the current:

  • Input 0. No voltage on the gate, so the transistor blocks. Current cannot reach ground, so the output node is pulled up to the supply through the resistor. Vout = 5 V = 1.
  • Input 1. Voltage on the gate, so the transistor conducts. It connects the output node straight to ground, and electricity takes that path. Vout = 0 V = 0.
A single transistor is a NOT gate

0 in gives 1 out. 1 in gives 0 out. You have not built anything yet and inversion has already appeared for free — it falls out of the wiring, because the transistor's job is to pull the output down.

This is worth holding onto. It is easy to assume the simplest gate must be AND or OR, since those are the ones that read like ordinary language. In actual silicon the simplest thing is the one that says no.

That asymmetry runs deeper than it looks. Because transistors naturally pull an output down, the gates that come cheapest in hardware are the inverting ones — NAND and NOR. A non-inverting AND is built by making a NAND and then inverting it, which costs more transistors, not fewer.

Keep that in your pocket. In section 2.3 you will learn that NAND alone can build every other gate, and it can look like an algebraic curiosity. It is not. It is a fact about what silicon does easily, and you have just watched where it comes from.

What to Carry Forward

Gates with more than one input work the same way, with more transistors. Wire two in series and current only reaches ground when both gates are on; wire them in parallel and either one is enough. Those two arrangements are already NAND and NOR — which is where the next section picks up.

We do not need to understand semiconductor physics to work with digital logic. We need exactly four things:

  1. A transistor is a voltage-controlled switch.
  2. Its output is a voltage on a wire, and the natural arrangement inverts — one transistor is a NOT gate.
  3. Transistors can be wired together, in series and in parallel, to form logic gates.
  4. Logic gates are the building blocks of all digital computation.

That is the last we will say about physics. From here on, the transistor is a switch and we build upward — at the gate level, with the transistors abstracted away.

2.2 — The Basic Gates

Logic gates are the vocabulary of digital computation. Every complex circuit — from a simple adder to a complete CPU — is built from combinations of a small set of basic gates. There are seven of them, and you will meet all seven on this page.

Start with the three most intuitive, because they map directly onto logic you already use when you write code.

The AND Gate

The AND gate outputs 1 if and only if all of its inputs are 1. Think of it as a circuit that requires every condition to be met.

In Boolean algebra, A AND B is written A · B or simply AB.

You already know this from programming

When you write a compound conditional in code — if (loggedIn && hasPermission) — you are describing an AND gate. The body runs only when both conditions are true. An AND gate is exactly that, in hardware: two wires in, one wire out, output high only when both inputs are high.

The || operator is an OR gate. The ! operator is a NOT gate. You have been programming with logic gates the whole time; you just were not holding one.

The OR gate outputs 1 if at least one of its inputs is 1.

The Inverted Gates: NAND, NOR, XNOR

Before the symbols, one piece of notation that unlocks four more gates at once.

A small circle on a gate's output means "inverted." That is the whole convention. An AND symbol with a circle is a NAND (NOT-AND). An OR symbol with a circle is a NOR (NOT-OR). Once you know that, you can read gates you have never seen before.

2.2.1 — Gates with More Than Two Inputs

Every truth table so far has had two inputs. Real gates are not limited to two, and neither is Logisim — when you place a gate you can set its input count to 3, 4, 5 or more. So what does a three-input AND gate do?

For most gates the answer is exactly what you would guess.

GateWith any number of inputs, outputs 1 when…
ANDevery input is 1
ORat least one input is 1
NANDat least one input is 0  (i.e. NOT all-ones)
NORevery input is 0
NOT— NOT always has exactly one input; there is nothing to generalize

Nothing surprising. A three-input AND needs all three high; a five-input OR needs only one.

XOR and XNOR are the odd ones — literally

With two inputs you can describe XOR as "the inputs are different," or equivalently "exactly one input is 1." Both descriptions give the right answer, so it is easy to assume they mean the same thing.

They do not. With three or more inputs the "exactly one" description breaks, and the correct rule is:

XOR outputs 1 when the sum of all its inputs is odd, and 0 when the sum is even.

XNOR is the mirror image: 1 when the sum is even, 0 when it is odd.

Check it against the two-input table above and you will see the two-input case was the parity rule all along: 0+1 = 1 (odd, output 1); 1+1 = 2 (even, output 0).

Where the "exactly one" idea falls apart

Take a three-input XOR with all three inputs set to 1:

  • "Exactly one input is 1" → false, so this rule would predict 0.
  • "The sum is odd" → 1 + 1 + 1 = 3, which is odd, so the parity rule predicts 1.

The gate outputs 1. The parity rule is right; the "exactly one" shortcut is wrong. Set the simulator below to XOR with three inputs and confirm it yourself.

This property has a name: XOR computes the parity of its inputs. It is genuinely useful — parity is the basis of the simplest error-detection schemes in memory and data transmission, where an extra bit is set to make the total number of 1s always odd (or always even) so a single flipped bit can be detected.

Forward reference

In Week 3 you will build a full adder, which adds three bits: two operands plus a carry-in. Its sum output is a three-input XOR — and now you know why. Adding three bits, the sum bit is 1 exactly when an odd number of them are 1. The parity rule is not a curiosity; it is how binary addition works.

One thing to watch in Logisim

A three-input NAND is not the same as chaining two two-input NANDs. A three-input NAND computes (ABC)' — the AND of all three, inverted once at the end. Chaining gives you ((AB)'C)', which is a different function.

When a circuit needs a three-input gate, set the gate's input count in its properties panel rather than wiring two gates together. The same caution applies to NOR.

What Comes Next

Forward reference

You now have all seven gates. That should raise a question: do we actually need seven?

AND, OR, and NOT are the gates that match how humans think, and the other four are conveniences built from them. But chip factories did not want seven kinds of gate, or even three. On the next page you will find out that one of these seven — the NAND gate — can be wired together to produce every one of the others, and therefore any circuit whatsoever, including a complete CPU.

That one gate type is sufficient for all of computing is the most important idea in this week, and arguably in this course.
