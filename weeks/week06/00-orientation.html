Week 6 — Seven Things at Once

The ALU, and the Words for It  |  09/21 – 09/27  |  Estimated time: ~8 hours

Where you are, and what is missing

What you built last week: a register. Eight cells with one shared write line, an output stage that can stop driving a shared wire, and two control pins that between them decide when a byte goes in and when it comes out. Two of them can hand a value to each other in four controlled steps.

What you can't do yet: change a byte. Every circuit you have built moves values or holds them. A register that holds 00101101 holds it forever unless something gives it a different byte — and nothing in your machine can produce one. A hundred registers wired together is a filing cabinet.

The same gap from the software side: you wrote add $t0, $t1, $t2 in MARS and it worked, but MARS is a Java program adding two numbers the way any program does. Your machine could not have executed that instruction. There is nothing in it that adds.

What you will be able to do by the end of this week: every transformation your CPU will ever perform on data — add, subtract, shift either way, invert, combine bitwise, compare — as circuits you built, and as instructions you can write.

The week the two tracks describe the same thing

Phase 2 runs hardware and assembly side by side, and last week they ran in parallel: you built a register, and separately you learned what an instruction set is. This week they stop being parallel.

Hardware

The seven operation legs of the ALU, each one built and tested on its own, plus the 8-bit comparator and the zero function. This is Logisim Checkpoint 2a.

Assembly

The MIPS instructions that ask for those exact operations: six R-type and four I-type, arithmetic and logic.

These are not two topics this week. They are one object seen from two ends.

add $t0, $t1, $t2 and the ALU's opcode-000 leg are the same operation described twice — once as something a program asks for, once as something a circuit does.

You will build the adder leg and then, two pages later, write the instruction that drives it. That is the whole point of interleaving the tracks, and this is the week where it pays.

What you build

  • AND_8BIT Eight AND gates side by side. Fifteen minutes, and it teaches something about speed that Week 15 spends a whole week on.
  • OR_8BIT The same, with OR gates.
  • NOT_8BIT Eight inverters on one input. Also half of how your machine subtracts.
  • ADDER_8BIT You already own this one. You built it in Week 3. Drop one instance in and the hardest leg is done.
  • SHIFT_LEFT Every bit moves up one place. One falls off the top, one arrives at the bottom.
  • SHIFT_RIGHT The mirror image.
  • XOR_CMP_1BIT One XOR gate producing three answers: the XOR result, whether two bits are equal, and which is larger.
  • XOR_CMP_8BIT Eight of those, chained — and chained in the opposite direction to your adder, for a reason worth understanding.
  • IS_ZERO An eight-input OR, inverted. The smallest circuit in the course, and one of the three signals that will let your CPU make a decision.
You finish this week with nine circuits and no ALU. That is correct.

Checkpoint 2a is the legs, built and tested individually. The part that chooses between them — the 3×8 decoder and the output-selection stage — is Checkpoint 2b, next week.

This is deliberate, and not only because of the holiday. Choosing turns out to be a subtler idea than any of the seven operations, it reuses ENABLE_8BIT from Week 5 in a way that deserves explaining properly, and it is the piece that would have been rushed if everything landed in one week.

So do not go looking for the part where it all comes together. It comes together in seven days, and the more thoroughly you test each leg on its own now, the less painful that will be.

A short week with a lot of building

Campus holiday, Friday 09/25

The week's load is about 8 hours rather than the usual 9, and roughly half of it is Logisim work rather than reading. That balance is different from any week so far.

Assignment 6 is 100 points across both tracks: Checkpoint 2a (50 points), four written questions, six instruction-to-hardware problems, and a MARS program.

Build each leg as you read about it, not all at the end

Every section on pages 1 and 2 is followed by a circuit, and the two are meant to interleave. Reading the whole week and then building the whole week works far less well — by the time you get to the comparator you will have lost the thread of why the adder chains one way and it chains the other.

There is also a practical reason. Six new circuits is more than any previous week, and six circuits built in one sitting is six chances to make the same wiring mistake before anybody notices.

Your tools this week

🔧
Logisim — same file, six new tabs
Open the same CSC210_YourName.circ you have been building since Week 2. This week it pays you back: ADDER_8BIT is already in there and you get the ALU's hardest leg by dragging one instance onto the canvas.
💻
MARS — the program layout for this course
.data first, then .text, no main: label and no .globl main. Execution starts at the first instruction in .text. Plenty of examples elsewhere use the other convention; mixing them inside one file is a reliable way to confuse yourself.
Which components you may use

Anything from Logisim's Wiring folder (pins, splitters, tunnels, constants, probes) and anything from Gates, including the controlled buffer you met last week. Nothing from Plexers, Arithmetic, or Memory.

The Arithmetic folder is the temptation this week — it contains an Adder, a Comparator and a Shifter, which is three of the nine circuits you are about to build. Using them would produce a working ALU that teaches nothing, and a student who takes that route discovers in Week 13 that they cannot debug a machine they did not build.

Splitters are going to be useful this week. If you have been avoiding them, page 1's shift legs are a good place to get comfortable.
