Week 15 — How Fast, and Who Built It

Performance; pipelining; macros, assemblers, linkers, loaders and debuggers  |  11/23 – 11/29  |  Estimated time: ~4 hours

Your CPU multiplies 5 × 5 in 462 steps. Is that fast?

Last week your CPU became a finished machine: it loads a program, runs it, and stops. Every week before this one asked how to build something. This week asks two questions about what you built. How fast is it, compared with the processor in your laptop, and why? And who built the program: what happens between the text you type and the bytes that sit in memory?

The first question needs three numbers and one equation, and leads to the idea every modern processor is built around: doing several instructions at once. The second names tools you have used since Week 5 (the assembler and the debugger) and two you have not met by name (the linker, and the loader, which you built in Week 14).

Week 15 class video: completing the CPU layout

About 29 minutes. This video is for the final project, not for this week’s four pages or the quiz: your instructor finishes the CPU layout begun in Week 14 and explains the special circuits around it in more detail than the written pages do. Nothing in Logisim is due this week, so watch it when you sit down to work on your CPU.

He opens with his finished CPU and control unit (01:47), why he keeps the wires spaced so they can be counted (02:12), and the control outputs down each side (03:37), including the difference between MAR_S and the MARS input pin (04:12), the END output (04:49), and the IO lines the course does not use (05:41).

Then the special circuits. Loading: the multiplexer, one of Week 14’s three built-in exceptions (06:39), set to 8 data bits (07:37) and switching between the counter and RAM input (08:17); the 256 counter, fed by load AND clock L (10:04), its subcircuit (10:30), the counter settings (11:03), its carry output (11:38), and the controlled buffer made left-handed (13:16). Carry-in: the one-bit Ctmp memory (15:01), the wire in the old diagram that goes nowhere (15:53), and why shifts need a carry-in (17:39). The clock: the clock E, S and L outputs (19:25), and gating the clock with END so a fast simulation stops when the program does (19:44).

He finishes with the two circuits you have to build and the tunnels (21:05), reset clearing RAM as well (21:35), MARS and load set by hand (22:13), reversed decoder bits as the most common wiring mistake (23:16), a preview of next week’s external RAM (23:52), and how to clear an all-red CPU by toggling reset in simulation mode (25:29), which works because reset is ORed into every set line (28:04).
