Clock Rate and Amdahl’s Law

Sections 15.5 – 15.8  |  Estimated time: ~30 minutes

15.5   What sets the clock

Page 1’s third lever was a shorter clock period. Why not make the period as short as you like? Because every step of your CPU is a transfer that has to settle before it is captured. In step 5 of an ADD, RA drives the bus, the bus drives the ALU, the adder works out the sum, and only then can ACC capture it. Your clock already allows for this: clk_e rises a quarter before clk_s, so every enabled path has a quarter to settle before anything is set (Week 11). Week 12 asked whether a memory could answer within that quarter.

On a real chip the same rule sets the clock. Between one register and the next there is combinational logic, and a signal takes time to pass through each gate. The critical path is the slowest such path, from a register’s output to another register’s input. The clock period must be at least the critical path’s delay plus the setup time of the register that captures the result (Week 4). Make the period any shorter and some register captures a value that has not finished arriving.

Logisim is different, honestly

Logisim does not simulate gate delays as time. It settles each change step by step and then reports the result, so your circuit has no critical path in nanoseconds. How fast it runs depends on how fast your computer can simulate it. Everything on this page is about real hardware, and your CPU’s quarters are the place where real hardware would need the time.

15.6   The ripple carry, again

In Week 6 you met the most important idea in computer performance while building AND_8BIT. Bit 3 of an AND depends only on bit 3 of its inputs, so all eight bits settle at once, one gate delay. Bit 3 of a sum depends on the carry from bit 2, which depends on bit 1, which depends on bit 0:

bit 0→ c1 →bit 1→ c2 →bit 2→ … →bit 7→ C

The answer ripples up through eight stages, one after another. Same eight bits, eight times slower, and the cause is not that addition is complicated: it is that each bit depends on the one before. Widen the adder to 16 bits and the chain doubles. On most processors the adder sits on the critical path, so the length of that chain helps set the clock for the whole machine.

Week 6 promised that this week you would meet the techniques real processors use to buy that time back, and that every one of them is an attempt to break a dependency. The first is below.

Going further: carry-lookahead (optional, not assessed)

Instead of waiting for the carry, compute it. Each bit position can say, from its own two inputs alone, whether it generates a carry (g = a AND b: both 1) or propagates one arriving from below (p = a OR b). Then each carry is a formula in the inputs only:

c1 = g0 + p0·c0 c2 = g1 + p1·g0 + p1·p0·c0 c3 = g2 + p2·g1 + p2·p1·g0 + p2·p1·p0·c0

Every carry is now two levels of gates from the inputs, all computed at the same time: the dependency is broken. The price is gates: each formula is longer than the last, and the AND gates need more and more inputs. Real adders therefore look ahead within blocks of four bits and ripple between the blocks. Your ALU uses the ripple-carry adder you built, and nothing in this course asks you to build this one.

15.7   Amdahl’s Law

Suppose you make one part of a program faster. How much faster is the whole program? If a fraction p of the original time is sped up by a factor s, and the rest is untouched:

speedup = 1 / ( (1 − p) + p / s ) as s grows without limit: speedup → 1 / (1 − p)

The untouched part, 1 − p, never shrinks, so it sets a ceiling however good the improvement is. That is Amdahl’s Law, and your CPU shows it clearly:

  • Fetch is half of every instruction. Three of every six steps fetch. Make execution infinitely fast and the program is still at most 1 / (1 − 0.5) = 2× faster.
  • Fetch in one step instead of three is s = 3 on p = 0.5: 1 / (0.5 + 0.5/3) = 1.5×.
  • MYSIM without its CLFs (page 1): 18 of 77 instructions removed entirely, so p = 18/77 and s = ∞: 1 / (1 − 18/77) = 77/59 = 1.30×, the same number page 1 got by counting.

The lesson designers draw from it is make the common case fast: effort spent on something that takes 5% of the time can never buy more than 5%.

15.8   Why not just raise the clock?

For decades, much of the speed-up in computers came from the third lever: each generation of chips had smaller transistors, which switched faster, so the clock went up. That stopped in the mid-2000s, at a few GHz, and has stayed there. Every clock tick switches transistors, and switching uses energy: run the clock faster and the chip uses more power and gets hotter, until it cannot be cooled. This limit is often called the power wall.

So designers turned to the other two levers: do more useful work in each cycle, and run more than one program at once. Page 3 is the first of those, and the main way a CPI below 1 becomes possible. Week 16 covers what a modern processor does with multiple cores and with memory.
