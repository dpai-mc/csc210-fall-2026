Week 7 — Selection, From Both Ends

Finishing the ALU, and MIPS control flow  |  09/28 – 10/04  |  Estimated time: ~9 hours

Both halves of this course are missing the same thing

Your hardware: nine circuits that transform bytes — add, shift either way, invert, combine bitwise, compare, test for zero. All of them compute, all the time, correctly. And nothing picks one. You have been reading the leg you wanted by hand.

Your software: a program that runs straight down the page. Every instruction executes, in order, exactly once. It cannot skip an instruction, cannot go back to one, and cannot call a procedure and come back. No if, no while, no functions.

Those look like two unrelated gaps. They are the same gap. Nothing chooses.

By the end of this week a 3-bit opcode selects one of seven answers out of a finished ALU, and your programs branch, loop, call procedures and return — including calling themselves.

This week is the opposite shape to every week since Week 3

One hour of hardware, six of software. Do not budget by habit.

Weeks 3 through 6 were hardware-heavy and you have probably settled into a rhythm around that. This week inverts it. The checkpoint is short — and the software side is the densest material in the course, and the part the Week 8 midterm leans on hardest.

What you build: nothing

Open the .circ file you have been adding to since Week 2. Everything Checkpoint 2b needs is already in it.

  • DECODER_3TO8 The opcode decoder. You built it in Week 3, alongside the 2×4 and the 4×16.
  • ENABLE_8BIT The output stage — you need seven copies. You built it in Week 5, as part of Checkpoint 1.
  • ADDER_8BIT Week 3.
  • SHIFT_LEFT, SHIFT_RIGHT, NOT_8BIT, AND_8BIT, OR_8BIT, XOR_CMP_8BIT, IS_ZERO Week 6.
Checkpoint 2b is one new tab, and everything in it is a part you already own

You will not build a decoder this week. You built three of them in Week 3, and one of them is exactly the size this needs. You will not build an output stage — ENABLE_8BIT was Checkpoint 1.

All that is new is wiring: instantiate, connect, name the result ALU_8BIT.

This is what Standards §4's rule — circuits are components for the final project, not disposable exercises — has been arranging since Week 3, and this is the fullest version of the payoff the course produces. Three weeks running, you have opened your file and found the hard part waiting.

The two tracks

Hardware — Checkpoint 2b

Assemble ALU_8BIT: the opcode decoder, seven enable blocks, an OR combine, the carry gating and the three flags. Verify all eight opcodes.

After this, the ALU does not change again for the rest of the course. It gets wired to things; it does not get rebuilt.

Assembly — control flow

The program counter. beq and bne, and the arithmetic that turns a label into an offset. Loops. j, jal, jr. The stack, the calling convention, and recursion.

Plus sll and srl, which close out the shift legs you built last week.

The week's best single fact: blt is not an instruction

MIPS has exactly two conditional branches that compare registers: equal, and not equal. blt, bgt, ble, bge — which you will find in every reference and every textbook — are expanded by the assembler into slt plus a branch.

And slt is answered by the AL flag off the comparator you chained together in Week 6.

if (a < b)  →  slt + bne  →  one wire out of an XOR cascade

That is a high-level language construct traced all the way down to gates you personally wired, and it is the clearest moment in the course where both halves meet. Page 3 is built on it.

Your tools this week

🔧
Logisim — one new tab, no new components
Same CSC210_YourName.circ. If a Week 6 leg was never quite right, deal with it before you assemble — a fault is much harder to find once seven parallel paths share an output.
💻
MARS — and leave delayed branching off
.data first, then .text, no main: label and no .globl main, as always. One new setting to be aware of: MARS has a "delayed branching" option that is off by default. Leave it off — with it on, the instruction after every branch and jump executes before the jump takes effect, and nothing in this course accounts for that.
Two registers to leave alone

$at — the assembler uses it as scratch space when it expands a pseudo-instruction. Page 2 explains this properly. Write to it yourself and an expansion two lines later will overwrite it without warning.

$sp — the stack pointer. Change it only in a matched pair, subtracting at the start of a procedure and adding the same amount back before returning. A procedure that returns with $sp in the wrong place breaks its caller, which is a miserable thing to debug.
