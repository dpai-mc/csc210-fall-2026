The Performance Equation

Sections 15.1 – 15.4  |  Estimated time: ~45 minutes

15.1   Three numbers

Your CPU runs MYSIM.txt and stops with 25 in R2. A laptop would multiply 5 by 5 so fast that you could not measure it with a stopwatch. To say how much faster, and why, you need a way to turn “fast” into a number. It takes three:

  • Instruction count (IC) — how many instructions the program executes. Not how many it contains: a loop body that runs eight times counts eight times.
  • Cycles per instruction (CPI) — how many clock cycles one instruction takes, on average.
  • Clock period (T) — how long one cycle lasts. Its reciprocal is the clock rate: a 1 GHz clock has a period of 1 ns.
Time = Instruction count × CPI × Clock period seconds = instructions × cycles/instruction × seconds/cycle

It is a product because instructions run one after another, each one taking CPI cycles, and each cycle taking T seconds. The units cancel to seconds, which is a good check that nothing is missing.

Week 9’s multi-cycle MIPS ran its trace program in 19 instructions and 76 cycles. If its clock ran at 1 GHz, the program took 19 × 4.0 × 1 ns = 76 ns. The equation does not care what the machine is, only these three numbers.

15.2   CPI is an average

Week 9’s machine does not take the same number of cycles for every instruction: a load uses memory twice and takes five, a branch takes three. So its CPI depends on the mix of instructions the program runs. Here is the trace program’s mix, counted by running it on the same model Week 9 used:

ClassExecutedCycles eachCycles

76 cycles over 19 instructions is a CPI of exactly 4.0, the number Week 9’s stepper reported. Change the program and the average changes: a loop full of loads has a higher CPI than a loop full of adds on the same machine. And anything that makes one class slower, such as Week 12’s wait states on a slow memory, raises the average by however often that class runs.

15.3   Your CPU

On your CPU, call one step one cycle. Every instruction is six steps (three to fetch, three to execute), whatever the instruction does. So your CPU’s CPI is 6, for every program, and the mix does not matter. One step is four ticks of the clock (the four quarters, Week 11), which is where the 24 clicks per instruction came from.

MYSIM.txt executes 77 instructions before END (Week 14 counted them the same way). That is 77 × 6 = 462 steps, or 1,848 ticks. Logisim 2.7.1’s fastest Tick Frequency setting is 4.1 KHz, which is 4,096 ticks a second, so one step lasts 4 / 4,096 s, about a millisecond:

Time = 77 × 6 × (4 / 4096 s) = 0.451 s

Your CPU multiplies 5 × 5 in under half a second, as long as Logisim can actually simulate your circuit that fast. A large circuit may not keep up with the setting, and how fast it really runs depends on the computer running Logisim. That is a description, not a diagnosis.

Where the steps go

CPI = 6 hides something. Not every instruction needs its six steps; some finish their work early and wait. The table counts, for each kind of instruction MYSIM.txt runs, how many of the six steps do any work (the rest are idle) — read from your control table, the same one Week 14 wired:

KindExecutedSteps that workIdle stepsSteps spent

CLF does all its work in step 4. JMP is done at step 5. A conditional jump that is not taken is done at step 5 too, because step 6 is the one that would have written the PC. Add up only the steps that work and MYSIM.txt needs 400 steps, not 462: a CPI of about 5.19, roughly 13% faster.

You do not build this

Getting those 62 steps back needs a stepper that resets early, after each kind of instruction’s last working step: more terms on the stepper’s reset, decided by the control unit. Week 9’s MIPS machine is built that way, which is why its instructions took 3, 4 or 5 cycles. Your CPU always runs all six because a fixed count keeps the stepper simple, and simple is what a CPU you build and debug in a semester needs (Week 11). It is a design choice with a measurable price, and now you have measured it.

15.4   Three levers

Each term of the equation is something you can change, and each belongs to a different part of the design:

LeverWho pulls itOn your CPU
Fewer instructionsThe programmer, the compiler, the instruction setA better algorithm; an instruction that does more
Fewer cycles per instructionThe control unitThe early-reset stepper in 15.3
A shorter cycleThe circuitPage 2

The instruction set: 18 CLFs

Of MYSIM.txt’s 77 instructions, 18 are CLF. They are there because your ADD and shifts take CTMP’s carry in (Week 14), so the program has to clear a stale carry before each one. An instruction set with an ADD that ignored the carry would not need them: 59 instructions, 354 steps, 1.30 × faster, with no change to the clock or the control unit. That is the trade every instruction-set designer makes: carry-in makes multi-byte arithmetic possible, and it costs every program that does not need it.

The algorithm: two ways to multiply

MYSIM.txt multiplies by shift-and-add: eight passes, one per bit of the multiplier, whatever the numbers are. The obvious alternative is repeated addition: add the multiplicand to a total, multiplier times. Both run on your CPU; here is what each costs, measured by running them:

MultiplyShift-and-addRepeated addition

For 5 × 5, repeated addition wins: 33 instructions against 77. Shift-and-add’s cost hardly moves (76 to 79 instructions, depending on how many 1 bits the multiplier has), while repeated addition’s grows by six instructions for every 1 added to the multiplier. At 12 × 15 they are nearly even, and from 13 on repeated addition loses. Fewer instructions is not a property of an algorithm alone; it depends on the inputs it will see. On a 32-bit machine, where the multiplier can be in the billions, shift-and-add wins by so much that it is what multiplier hardware does.

The clock is the third lever, and it is the subject of the next page. How far all three can go: a laptop core today runs at a few GHz, and completes several instructions in a single cycle, a CPI below 1. Page 3 starts to show how a CPI below your CPU’s 6, and below 1, is possible at all.
