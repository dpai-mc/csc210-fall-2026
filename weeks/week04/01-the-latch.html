Feedback: The S-R Latch

Sections 4.1 – 4.3  |  Estimated time: ~2.25 hours

Last week ended with a promise. You had just built an adder and three decoders — real components, correct, reusable — and the closing page pointed out the one thing none of them could do: remember. Change the inputs and the answer is gone. Not stored badly, not stored somewhere hard to reach. There is nowhere in a combinational circuit for a value to have been kept.

This page keeps that promise, and it keeps it with two NAND gates.

Not a new component. Not a memory chip. Two of the gates you built in Week 2, wired so that each one's output feeds the other one's input. That loop holds a bit. It will keep holding it after you let go of the input, and it will keep holding it until you deliberately change it — which is the entire definition of memory.

4.1   Why Nothing So Far Remembers

Every circuit you have built in this course has the same property, and it is worth stating precisely before we break it:

The combinational property

The output is a function of the present input, and of nothing else. Give the circuit the same inputs twice and it gives you the same answer twice — no matter what happened in between.

That sounds like a guarantee, and in a sense it is. It is also a wall.

What it rules out

Take your 8-bit adder. It adds two numbers. Now add three numbers.

You cannot. There is nowhere to put the first sum while you go and fetch the third number. The adder does not have a "hold this for a moment" — the instant you change its inputs to present the third number, the first sum is gone. Not overwritten, not moved. It was never anywhere; it was just the shape the wires happened to be making.

The same wall stops everything else:

  • You cannot count. Counting means knowing what number you were on.
  • You cannot do one thing and then another. "And then" requires remembering that the first thing happened.
  • You cannot run a program. A program is a sequence, and a sequence is nothing but "and then" repeated.

Without memory there is no such thing as software. There is only a fixed shape that transforms whatever is presented to it — very fast, entirely correct, and unable to have a second thought.

What the missing ingredient is not

Before going further, rule two things out, because both are the natural first guess.

It is not a new kind of gate. Week 2 proved NAND is universal — every logic function can be built from NAND alone. So if memory were a logic function, you could already build it. You cannot, which means it is not one.

It is not more gates. Any arrangement of gates where signals flow from inputs to outputs is still combinational, no matter how many gates it contains. A million of them in a row is still a shape.

Whatever memory is, it is not a bigger truth table

Both dead ends have the same cause: in every circuit so far, signals travel forwards. Inputs on the left, outputs on the right, and nothing ever comes back.

Which leaves exactly one structural option nobody has tried. What if the circuit's own output were one of its inputs?

Optional: a device you already own that has this problem solved

This aside assumes nothing but everyday experience — no programming background, nothing from another course. It is here because it makes the idea land faster for some people. Skipping it costs you nothing; the explanation above and below is complete on its own, and no quiz question or assignment problem depends on having read this.

A wall light switch is combinational. The switch position is the state: flick it up and the light is on, flick it down and it is off. You can tell what the lamp is doing by looking at the switch, because the switch holds no history — it just is where it is.

A TV power button is not like that. It is the same button whether the TV is on or off, and what it does depends on what happened before. Press it when the TV is off and it turns on. Press the identical button again and it turns off. The button has no idea what it is doing; the television is remembering, and the button is just an event.

You have owned a device with state your whole life. What this week adds is the answer to how.

Where this analogy breaks — and these matter:

  • A TV button is a toggle. The latch is not. The circuit you are about to build has separate set and reset inputs, and setting an already-set latch does nothing the second time. Do not expect the latch to flip back and forth from one input.
  • One input versus two. The button is a single control. The latch has two, and which one you drive decides which way it goes.
  • It explains the behaviour, not the mechanism. The TV's state lives inside a chip that is itself full of latches — so the analogy is describing the thing we are trying to explain, from the outside. The mechanism is the entire subject of 4.2, and there the analogy has nothing to offer.

4.2   Feedback: The S-R Latch

Here is the circuit. Two NAND gates. Each one's output is wired into the other one's input.

The NAND S-R latch. The two heavy wires in the middle are the whole idea: each gate's output is the other gate's input, so the circuit's present state is part of what determines its present state.

That is the entire circuit. Two gates and two wires that go backwards. Everything below is a consequence of those two wires.

Angle 1 — the circuit, operated

The inputs are called nS and nR — "not-S" and "not-R" — and they are active LOW. That means you activate them by driving them to 0, not to 1.

Active LOW, and the naming rule for the rest of the course

A pin name beginning with a lowercase n is active LOW. Drive nS to 0 to set. Drive nR to 0 to reset. Leaving both at 1 does nothing — and "does nothing" is about to turn out to be the most interesting row in the table.

Why write it this way? Because a Logisim pin name cannot carry an overbar, and an apostrophe is a nuisance to type in every later week that uses the component. The n prefix is what this course uses from here on.

This is the single most common source of confusion this week. If a result surprises you, check the polarity first.

Walk the four combinations by tracing signals rather than reciting a table. Remember the one rule a NAND obeys: its output is 0 only when all of its inputs are 1. Any 0 on an input forces the output to 1.

Set (nS=0, nR=1). A 0 into the upper gate forces its output q to 1, whatever else is happening. That 1 travels down the feedback wire into the lower gate, which now sees 1 and 1, so nq goes to 0. And that 0 travels back up into the upper gate — which is already outputting 1 and is now being told to, twice over.

Reset (nS=1, nR=0) is the same story with the gates swapped: nq is forced to 1, that 1 feeds the upper gate which now sees 1 and 1, so q goes to 0.

The row that is not like the others

Now the hold state: both inputs at 1. Neither gate is being forced to do anything. So what does the circuit do?

Suppose q is 1 and nq is 0. Check it for consistency: the lower gate sees nR=1 and the fed-back q=1 — two ones — so it outputs 0. That is nq=0, which is what we assumed. The upper gate sees nS=1 and the fed-back nq=0 — a zero on an input — so it outputs 1. That is q=1, which is also what we assumed. The configuration is self-consistent. Nothing wants to change.

Now suppose the opposite: q is 0 and nq is 1. The upper gate sees nS=1 and nq=1, so it outputs 0 — that is q=0. The lower gate sees nR=1 and q=0, so it outputs 1 — that is nq=1. Also self-consistent. Also nothing wants to change.

Two stable states under identical inputs

With nS=1 and nR=1, this circuit has two configurations it can sit in forever, and the inputs do not choose between them.

So the inputs alone do not determine the output. Something else does — and the only thing left is which of the two states the loop settled into earlier. That is where the bit is. Not in a component, not in a special material. The bit is which stable state this loop is currently in, and it stays there because each gate keeps driving the value that makes the other gate keep driving it.

Memory is feedback. There is no memory ingredient. There is a loop.

That paragraph is the most important one in this course. Everything from here — registers, RAM, the program counter, the whole notion of a machine that executes one instruction and then the next — is that idea, replicated.

The table has a hole in it, and the hole is the memory

Every truth table you built in Weeks 2 and 3 could be filled in completely from the inputs alone. That was the definition of the thing you were building.

This one cannot. The first row's output is not a value, it is the word holds — because writing a 0 or a 1 there would require knowing something the inputs do not tell you. The table is not incomplete by accident. It is incomplete because the circuit is no longer a function of its inputs, and that is precisely what we came here for.

Angle 3 — and where the physical intuition went

Section 4.1 offered an optional aside about a TV power button: the same press does different things depending on what happened before. If you read it, this is the moment it pays off — and if you skipped it, nothing here depends on it.

The button showed you that devices with a past exist and that you already own several. This circuit is how that is done. Not a metaphor for it, not an approximation of it — the actual mechanism, two gates and a wire that goes backwards, at the bottom of every memory you have ever used.

4.3   The Forbidden State, and What It Teaches

Drive both inputs to 0 at once. A 0 on an input forces a NAND's output to 1, so both gates output 1 — and q and nq are both 1 at the same time.

Look at what those names promise. nq is supposed to be the complement of q. Right now it is not. The circuit has not broken and nothing is on fire; it is doing exactly what two NAND gates with a 0 on each of them must do. But it is in a state where its own labels are lying, and any circuit downstream that trusts nq to be the opposite of q is now being told something false.

The state itself is not really the problem

While you hold both inputs at 0, the latch sits there stably. It is useless — it is not storing anything, both outputs are 1 — but it is not dangerous.

The problem is leaving.

The race

Release both inputs at the same moment. Both go from 0 back to 1.

Now both gates are looking at each other's 1 and both are trying to go to 0 — and the instant either one succeeds, it forces the other one back to 1. Whichever gate gets there first wins, and the loop settles with that gate's output low.

Which gate gets there first is not determined by anything in the design. It comes down to which piece of silicon happens to switch a fraction of a nanosecond sooner — manufacturing variation, temperature, the length of a wire. Two copies of the identical circuit can land differently. The same copy can land differently on two different runs.

Try it in the bench above with the Release both at once preset. Step through it. It does not settle — the values oscillate and never reach a fixed point, because the honest simulation of "both gates change together and neither goes first" has no answer to give you. That is not the widget avoiding the question. There is no answer.

What Logisim will show you, and why it is not your fault

When you build this circuit and press run, the outputs may flicker between red error states and apparently random values before settling — and they may settle differently on different runs.

A real latch has no defined state at power-on. Both gates race, one wins on tiny physical differences, and the circuit lands in a stable but arbitrary state. Logisim showing you red is Logisim being honest that nothing has determined the answer yet.

This is also why real sequential systems carry a global reset that forces every memory element into a known state before anything else happens.

And it is what the pull-down setting on your input pins is for. The assignment asks you to set both inputs to 2-state with pull-down behaviour, so they come up at 0 after a reset. Notice what that means for this circuit: 0 on both inputs is the forbidden state, so your raw latch will come up flickering. That is expected, and page 2's circuit is where that stops being true.

The lesson, which is why this is a section and not a footnote

The obvious response to a bad state is a rule: never drive both inputs low. Write it in the documentation, tell the students, move on.

You do not fix a race by being careful

A rule is a promise made by a person. The circuit cannot read it, cannot check it, and will do exactly what its wires say the moment something upstream glitches, or a signal arrives a nanosecond early, or a future student wires it up differently.

The engineering answer is not to avoid the bad state. It is to build a circuit that cannot reach it — where the forbidden combination is not forbidden by instruction but impossible by construction.

That is the next page, and it is also where this circuit turns into the one your CPU is actually made of.

One more thing this circuit is, and is not

The latch you have just built is asynchronous: it responds the moment an input changes, with no clock involved anywhere. That is unusual for this course. From page 2 onward, every component you build changes only when something explicitly tells it to.

That is not an inconsistency to be tidied away later — it is the order the ideas have to come in. Memory has to exist before it can be scheduled. Showing you the unscheduled version first is what makes the next two pages look like solutions rather than conventions.
