What This Session Is, and What It Is Not

This lab does not assemble the ALU, and it does not write the assignment's procedures. Checkpoint 2b and the four procedures of Part D are Assignment 7, built from the assignment page on your own time. The build steps, the test values and the check table are there, not here.

What we build here is an instrument and a program that nothing grades. BRANCH_BENCH takes one of your Week 6 comparators and turns its two flags into an answer for every branch you can write — real or pseudo. Then, in MARS, you write a small procedure pair of your own, watch the program counter jump and the stack move, and break the calling convention twice on purpose to see what each rule is protecting.

The Question Today Answers

What you have: A comparator from Week 6 with two outputs, al and eq. This week's learning pages say those two wires are where every MIPS comparison ends up: blt becomes slt plus a branch, and slt is answered by the comparator.

What you cannot do yet: Say, for any pair of bytes, which of the six comparison branches would be taken — and whether your comparator and MIPS would agree about it. Last week you found that the adder has two readings of every byte and computes only one. Is the comparator any different?

What you can do after today: Read a branch condition straight off two flags, know exactly which pairs of numbers your comparator and MIPS disagree on and why, and point at the stack in MARS and say what each saved word is for.

What You Will Be Able to Do

  1. Build a test bench that turns a comparator's two flags into the answer for all six comparison branches.
  2. Measure the comparator against MIPS on six pairs, and find the pairs where they disagree.
  3. Design a two-gate fix that makes an unsigned comparator answer a signed question.
  4. Write a leaf and a non-leaf procedure to a specification, and read the program counter, $ra and the stack as the program runs.
  5. Break the calling convention on purpose and say which rule each break violated.

Part 2 — Build BRANCH_BENCH ~12 minTogether

Two bytes in. Two flags from your comparator. Six outputs, one per comparison branch, each reading 1 exactly when that branch would be taken for a and b.

  1. Add two 8-bit input pins, a and b, on the left. On each, add two probes: one with Radix set to unsigned decimal, one to signed decimal — as on last week's bench.
  2. Place one XOR_CMP_8BIT instance from the project pane. a into its a, b into its b. Leave its xor output unconnected; today uses only the two flags.
  3. Add six 1-bit output pins, named exactly blt, bgt, ble, bge, beq and bne. Each will read 1 when that branch, with a as its first register and b as its second, would be taken.

The condition gates — you design these

Your comparator gives you two facts: al says a is larger than b, and eq says a equals b. Every one of the six conditions is a statement about those two facts. Write each as a sentence first — “a is less than b exactly when a is not larger and …” — then turn the sentences into gates.

3
Six outputs from two wires

Two of the six need no gate at all — they are one of the flags, straight through. The other four each need one gate from the Gates folder. Work out all six on the observation sheet's design table before you place anything.

Stuck on blt? Open this after you have tried.

If a is not larger than b, and a is not equal to b, there is only one possibility left. You want a 1 when both flags are 0, and there is a single gate whose truth table is exactly that.

The learning page puts it the other way round: slt $at, $t0, $t1 asks the comparator whether $t1 is larger than $t0 — the same flag with the operands swapped. Your gate and the page's swap give the same answer. You will test that claim in Part 4.

Verify before going on — two minutes
  1. a = 00000111, b = 00000111. Exactly three outputs read 1: ble, bge and beq.
  2. a = 00000001, b = 00001000. Exactly three outputs read 1, and blt is one of them.

If more than three outputs light for any pair, two of your conditions overlap where they should not — check the sentence you wrote for each.

Part 3 — Six Pairs, Bench Against MIPS ~8 minYour own, then compare

For each row of the observation sheet, set a and b and record al, eq and your bench's blt. Then fill in the last column yourself, by thinking rather than by poking: would MIPS's blt branch? MIPS compares registers as signed two's-complement numbers, so use the signed reading of each byte.

The inputs are given with both of their readings so you do not have to convert them. Do all six rows before looking for a pattern.

Compare With the Room — before you poke row 4

a = 11111110, b = 00000011. Will your bench's blt read 1? Everybody answers yes or no, out loud or in the chat, before anyone sets the pins. Then set them.

4
Find the rule

Mark every row where your bench's blt and MIPS's blt disagree. Then finish this sentence on the observation sheet: my comparator and MIPS disagree exactly when … Look at bit 7 of a and bit 7 of b on the rows you marked, and on the rows you did not.

Then one more: which reading of the bytes is your comparator actually using? Your probes will tell you.

Part 4 — Fix It, Then Break It ~10 minYour own, then compare

Your comparator is not wrong. It answers the question it was built to answer. The trouble is that MIPS's slt asks a different one — and the difference only shows on some pairs. Here is a fix that costs two gates and a second comparator, and that you design.

5
Make an unsigned comparator answer a signed question

Look again at your marked rows. On every row where the comparator was wrong, it was wrong for the same reason, and the reason lives entirely in one bit of each operand. Something done to those two bits — the same thing to each — before the comparator sees them makes it right on every row.

Build it: splitters on a and b, your change on the two bits, the bytes merged back, and a second XOR_CMP_8BIT instance fed with the changed bytes. From its al and your original eq, make one more 1-bit output named sblt, the same way you made blt.

Run all six rows again and record sblt. It should agree with MIPS on every one.

Stuck on the sign fix? Open this after you have tried.

In an unsigned byte, bit 7 set means large. In a signed byte, bit 7 set means negative — the smallest numbers there are. The comparator's ordering is right about bits 6 to 0 in both readings; it only has bit 7 backwards. So turn bit 7 around on both operands — one NOT gate each — and the order the comparator computes becomes the signed order.

A
Break it: swap the operands

Swap the two wires into your first comparator, so that b goes into its a and a into its b. Run row 1 and row 3 again and record all six branch outputs.

Which outputs changed, and which did not? Record which ones traded places with each other. Then put the wires back.

In MIPS, slt $at, $t0, $t1 and slt $at, $t1, $t0 differ by nothing but this. It is the most common single error in hand-written comparisons, and it assembles without complaint.

Part 5 — MARS: Watch the PC Jump and the Stack Move ~55 minTogether, then your own

The session turns ninety degrees. Your bench answers whether a branch would be taken; MARS will show you what taking it does — a new value written into the program counter — and what a procedure call does to $ra and to the stack.

Write a program — the specification, not the code

Your Program

Write a MIPS program, in one file named lab07_yourname.asm, with two procedures and a top level:

  1. classify — takes $a0 and $a1, returns in $v0: −1 if $a0 is less than $a1, 0 if they are equal, 1 if $a0 is greater. It is a leaf. It compares with slt and beq/bne only — no blt, bgt or the rest of that family — and its first slt writes $t0. It may use $t0, $t1 and $v0, and nothing else.
  2. report — takes $a0 and $a1, calls classify, and prints one line: the first number, a space, one character (<, = or >), a space, the second number, and a newline. Because it calls another procedure and needs its arguments after that call, it needs a stack frame: save $ra, $a0 and $a1 on entry, restore them before returning, and return $sp to where it started.
  3. The top level calls report six times, with the six pairs from your observation sheet as signed numbers — the first pair is 45 and 18 — then exits cleanly.

Constraints: .data first (it may be empty), then .text, no main: label and no .globl main. Leave MARS's delayed branching off (it is off by default). Pseudo-instructions other than the blt family are permitted. Comment every executable line, and put a comment block above each procedure saying whether it needs a frame and why.

It is thirty-five to forty-five instructions. The part that needs thought is report's frame — how many words, which offsets, and which order to restore them in.

Printing Uses $a0 and $v0

Every print puts its argument in $a0 and its service number in $v0. So report destroys its own first argument the moment it prints anything, and destroys classify's answer the moment it sets up a print. Decide where each value lives before you write the prints.

Run it, and watch

  1. Assemble (F3) and run (F5). You should get six lines. Check each against the MIPS column of your observation sheet.
  2. Reset (F12). In the Text Segment pane, tick the Bkpt box on your first jal report, and run to it.
  3. Record the address of that jal (the Address column), and $sp. Step once (F7). Record pc and $ra.
  4. Step through report's prologue — the stack adjustment and the three stores. Record $sp again. In the Data Segment pane, choose current $sp from the drop-down and find your three saved words. Record which address holds which.
  5. Keep stepping into classify. Find its first beq or bne in the Text Segment pane and record its address, the address of the label it branches to, and the low four hex digits of its Code column — that is the 16-bit offset field.

Two breaks — run each, record it, undo it

B
Forget to save $ra

In report, comment out the one line that saves $ra and the one line that restores it. Nothing else. Before running, vote: how many lines will print?

Run it. When you have seen enough, press Stop. Record what printed, and — stepping from the start if you need to — the address that report's jr $ra jumped to. Then uncomment both lines.

C
Keep an argument in a $t register

In report, instead of saving $a0 on the stack, copy it into $t0 before the jal classify, and print the first number from $t0 afterwards. Leave the frame otherwise as it was.

It assembles. Run it and record the six lines. Then put it back.

Three questions — answer them in the comments of your .asm

These are the point of the exercise.

Q1
Your comparator against MIPS

Your program printed MIPS's answer for all six pairs. On which rows did your bench's blt disagree with it, and which of your two bench outputs — blt or sblt — agreed with MIPS on every row?

Then one sentence: which reading of a byte does your Week 6 comparator compute, and which does MIPS's slt compute?

Q2
Where did jr $ra go?

Using the addresses you recorded, say what $ra held when break B's report reached jr $ra, which instruction that address belongs to, and why the first line printed correctly but the second never did.

Q3
What were those numbers?

Break C printed wrong first numbers. Say exactly what each of them was — where it came from in classify — and name the rule of the calling convention that break violated. Then say where a value that must survive a call is allowed to live.

If You Finish Early

Write countdown: it takes $a0 = n, prints n and a space, calls itself with n − 1 if n is greater than 0, and then prints n and a space again before returning. Call it once from the top level with 3. Predict the output before you run it.

Then set a breakpoint on the line that prints the second time, and read $sp the first time it stops. How many frames are on the stack at that moment, and what does each one hold? This is not graded; recursion is the same jal, $ra and frame you just wrote, applied to a procedure that happens to call itself.

The Two Halves, Joined

This morning you found that your comparator, like last week's adder, sees one reading of a byte — and that on the pairs where the sign bits differ, that reading gives the opposite answer from MIPS. Two NOT gates fixed it.

MIPS has the same choice to make, and makes it per instruction. The slt you used this afternoon is the signed question. MIPS also has an unsigned one, which is not on this course's list; your Week 6 comparator, on its own, is that one. Deciding which question your CPU's comparisons ask is a design decision, and now you know what it costs in gates.

Two resources worth bookmarking: the MARS repository, and Amell Peralta's MIPS assembly playlist, whose conventions this course follows.
