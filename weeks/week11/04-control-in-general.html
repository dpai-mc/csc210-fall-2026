Control Units in General

Sections 11.13 – 11.16  |  Estimated time: ~1 hour

11.13   Yours is hardwired

Step back from the gates. Your control unit is a function: its inputs are the step the stepper is on and the bits of the instruction, and its output is a set of control lines. Page 3’s control table is that function written out. Whatever processor you look at, from your 8-bit CPU to the one in your phone, its control unit computes some function like this one. Processors differ in how big the table is, and in how they store it.

Your CPU stores it as gates. Each line is an OR of AND terms — flags_s = (step5 AND ALU) OR (step4 AND CLF), then ANDed with clk_s — and the function is the wiring. This is hardwired control. It is fast, because a line is only a few gate delays from its inputs, and it is rigid: to change what an instruction does you rewire.

A control unit is often described as “just a combinational circuit”. Yours is a combinational circuit plus the stepper. The gates on page 3 forget instantly; the stepper is what lets the same instruction byte mean “RB to TMP” at one moment and “ACC to RB” two steps later. Every multi-step control unit has both parts: memory that holds where it is, and logic that turns where it is into lines.

11.14   Microprogrammed control

There is another way to store a table: as a table. Give every combination of instruction class and step an address, and store at that address a word with one bit per control line. Each step, read the word and send its bits to the lines. The words are called microinstructions, the table a microprogram, and the memory a control store — usually a ROM.

Real processors mix the two. A modern x86 handles its common instructions with fast hardwired decoding and keeps a microcode ROM for the rare, complicated ones, and its manufacturers ship microcode updates to fix bugs in chips that are already in computers. For eight lines and three classes, gates are simpler than a ROM, which is why your CPU is hardwired.

    11.15   Single-cycle and multi-cycle

    Your control unit has a stepper because one instruction takes several transfers on one bus. Not every processor works that way. In Week 9 you met the alternative: a single-cycle MIPS, which does a whole instruction in one long clock cycle, with a separate instruction memory and data memory and separate adders so that nothing has to be used twice. Its control unit has no step input at all. The lines depend only on the opcode, so the whole control unit is one combinational truth table:

    X means “don’t care”: sw and beq write no register, so it does not matter which register would have been chosen or what would have been written. The lines mean:

    • RegDst — which instruction field names the register written: rd (1, R-type) or rt (0, I-type).
    • ALUSrc — the ALU’s second operand: a register (0) or the sign-extended immediate (1).
    • MemtoReg — what is written to the register: the ALU result (0) or the word read from memory (1).
    • RegWrite, MemRead, MemWrite — whether the register file is written, and whether data memory is read or written.
    • Branch — this is a beq: if the ALU’s zero output is 1, the next PC is the branch target.
    • ALUOp — two bits telling a small second control unit how to set the ALU: add (00, for addresses and addi), subtract (01, to compare for beq), or look at the funct field (10, R-type).

    Three of those lines — RegDst, ALUSrc and MemtoReg — are not enables or sets. They are multiplexer selects: each chooses which of two values flows on to the next part. That is the job multiplexers do in a processor, and your Week 3 rule still holds: decoders select, multiplexers route. MIPS’s control decides with logic like yours; its datapath routes data with multiplexers. Your single-bus CPU routes data by choosing which register drives the bus instead, so it has no multiplexers at all.

    The price of single-cycle is the clock. Every instruction gets the same one cycle, so the cycle must be long enough for the slowest instruction — lw, which reads the register file, adds, reads data memory and writes a register, all in sequence. A beq that finishes far sooner still waits out the whole cycle.

    Multi-cycle splits an instruction into short steps and lets instructions take different numbers of them. Week 9’s MIPS took 3 cycles for a branch and 5 for a lw; its control unit was a finite state machine, a state register saying which cycle it is on, plus logic choosing the lines and the next state. Your CPU is multi-cycle too, and its state register is the stepper. The difference is that your stepper always runs all six steps.

    11.16   What your CPU costs

    Count it. Every instruction is six steps, every step four quarters: 24 clicks of clk_in, whatever the instruction does. CLF does all its work in step 4 and then waits through steps 5 and 6. A compare does nothing in step 6. When fetch arrives in Week 13, three of every instruction’s six steps will go to fetching it.

    None of that is a mistake. A fixed six steps keeps the stepper and its step7-to-reset wire simple, and simple is what a CPU you can build and debug in a semester needs. But it has a measurable cost, and in Week 15 you will measure it, in cycles per instruction — the number that Week 9’s MIPS stepper reported as 4.0 for its trace program. Your CPU’s figure is 6 steps for every instruction, so the only way to make it faster is a faster clock or fewer instructions.
