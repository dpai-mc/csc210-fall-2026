A Program That Computes

Sections 6.12 – 6.13  |  Estimated time: ~0.7 hours

6.12   Ten instructions, one program

Last week's MARS program moved values around and printed one. This one computes with them. Type it in, assemble it, and single-step through it — every instruction in it is one you met on page 3, and every one of them is a leg you built.

The program layout for this course

.data comes first, then .text. There is no main: label and no .globl main. Execution starts at the first instruction in .text.

You will find plenty of MIPS examples online, and in older material, that open with .globl main and a main: label. That style exists because of how programs get linked together in a full toolchain, and MARS accepts it — but this course does not use it, the video playlist you were pointed at does not use it, and mixing the two conventions inside one file is a reliable way to produce a program that assembles and then does not run the way you expect. Pick this layout and keep it for the rest of the course.

.data alpha: .word 250 beta: .word 175 gamma: .word 68 .text lw $t0, alpha # $t0 = 250 lw $t1, beta # $t1 = 175 lw $t2, gamma # $t2 = 68 add $s0, $t0, $t1 # 250 + 175 = 425 -- ADD leg sub $s0, $s0, $t2 # 425 - 68 = 357 -- NOT leg, then ADD with carry-in 1 andi $s1, $s0, 0x00FF # keep the low byte -- AND leg, immediate ZERO-extended ori $s2, $s0, 0x0F00 # set bits 8-11 -- OR leg, immediate ZERO-extended and $t3, $s1, $s2 # register form of the same leg or $t4, $s1, $s2 # register form of the same leg nor $s3, $s1, $s2 # OR leg, then NOT leg slt $s4, $s1, $s2 # is $s1 < $s2? -- comparator AL flag slti $s5, $s1, 100 # is $s1 < 100? -- same flag, immediate SIGN-extended addi $t5, $s1, -1 # ADD leg, immediate SIGN-extended li $v0, 1 # service 1: print integer add $a0, $s0, $zero # copy $s0 into $a0 by adding zero to it syscall li $v0, 10 # service 10: exit syscall
Two small things in there worth noticing

add $a0, $s0, $zero is how you copy a register. MIPS has no move instruction, and it does not need one: $zero always reads as 0, so adding it to something and storing the result somewhere else is a copy. You will see MARS accept move $a0, $s0 — that is a pseudo-instruction, and the assembler turns it into exactly the line above. Writing it out yourself once is worth doing.

andi and ori use the same 16-bit field and both zero-extend it, while slti and addi two lines below sign-extend theirs. Same format, same field, opposite treatment — and the -1 in the last addi is the one that would break if it were zero-extended.

$s3 is the interesting row

It is negative, and nothing in the program said anything about negative numbers. nor inverted every bit of a small positive value, which turns all the leading zeros into leading ones — and a leading 1 in two's complement is a negative number.

The bits are exactly what you would expect; the reading is the surprise. MARS is showing you a signed interpretation of a result that was never meant to be a number at all. This is the Week 1 point about a byte meaning whatever its context says it means, arriving a second time in a place where it actually matters.

Step it, do not just run it

Assemble, then use single-step and watch the register window after each instruction rather than only at the end. Two things are worth watching for specifically: the moment sub executes (the value changes as if a subtractor existed, and you know one does not), and the moment slt executes (a whole register becomes 1, from a single wire).

6.13   One machine, two halves

Take stock of what this week actually added.

Hardware

  • Three bitwise legs: AND, OR, NOT
  • The adder leg — instantiated, not built
  • Two shift legs
  • A one-bit comparator, chained into an eight-bit one
  • The zero function

Assembly

  • Six R-type instructions: add sub and or nor slt
  • Four I-type: addi andi ori slti
  • Sign-extension versus zero-extension
  • A program that computes rather than one that moves
You now own every transformation your CPU will ever perform on data

Not most of them. All of them. Weeks 8 through 17 add memory, a control unit, a datapath and a program counter — but nothing in any of those weeks does anything to a byte that is not on the list above. Every arithmetic and logical operation your finished machine can perform is a leg you built this week.

The rest of the course is about getting the right bytes to those legs, at the right moment, in the order a program asks for.

What you cannot do yet, and it is one thing twice

Your seven legs all compute, all the time, and every one of their answers reaches the same place. Nothing chooses. You have been choosing by hand all week — setting an opcode yourself and reading the leg you wanted.

And your program runs straight down the page. Every instruction executes, in order, exactly once. It cannot skip anything and it cannot repeat anything, which means it cannot make a decision or a loop. Nothing chooses.

Week 7 — selection, from both directions

Hardware. The 3×8 decoder and the output-selection stage, which together make your nine circuits into one ALU_8BIT. That is Logisim Checkpoint 2b, and it is where ENABLE_8BIT from Week 5 comes back — seven of them, one per leg.

Assembly. Branches and jumps: instructions whose effect is to change which instruction runs next. They are how an if and a while exist at all, and the wire they read is the EQ flag off the comparator you built this week.

Two tracks, one idea, arriving in the same week. That is not a coincidence — it is the same missing capability seen from the two ends, which is what this whole phase of the course is built to show you.

Before you go: your ALU is not assembled, and it should not be

You finish this week with nine separate circuits and no ALU. That is the checkpoint, in full, and it is not an unfinished job.

Test each leg on its own now, while it is a small circuit with four pins that you can reason about completely. Next week seven of them get wired to one output with a decoder in between, and a leg with a quiet fault in it becomes very much harder to find.
