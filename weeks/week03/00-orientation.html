Week 3: These Are Your Parts

Estimated time for this page: ~30 minutes  |  Estimated time for the full week: ~9 hours  |  Topic: Combinational Logic

What We Have / What We Can't Do / What We Can Now Do

What you learned last week: Individual logic gates that compute simple functions. You know all seven gates, you know NAND is universal, and you have the Boolean algebra to prove why. You have built circuits in Logisim from primitive gates.

What you can't do yet: Compute anything useful. A single gate answers one yes-or-no question about one or two bits. Nothing you have built can add two numbers, decide which of several values to act on, or work with a multi-bit quantity as a whole. Gates are the alphabet; you cannot yet write a word.

What you will be able to do by the end of this week: Build circuits that perform real arithmetic and real selection on 8-bit values. You will build an adder that adds two 8-bit numbers, and decoders that turn a binary code into a one-of-N selection. These are not exercises — they are the first components of the CPU you will finish this course with, and you will use them unchanged for the rest of the semester.

Last week ended with a proof: one gate type can build everything. This week collects on it. You will spend most of Week 3 wiring gates you already understand into arrangements that do something a gate cannot do alone.

Nothing new is added to the toolbox. The adder is XOR, AND, and OR. The decoder is NOT and AND. That is worth saying plainly at the start, because it is the point:

Complexity in hardware comes from composition, not from new primitives.

These Are Parts, Not Exercises

Something changes this week, and it is worth understanding before you start rather than discovering in Week 6.

Weeks 1 and 2 were preparation. The circuits you built in Week 2 — NOT from a NAND, AND from two NANDs — were there to prove a point, and once the point was made you were free to forget them.

Week 3 is where this course stops doing exercises and starts building the machine

Every circuit you build this week is a component of the CPU you finish this course with. Not practice for it. A part of it.

The FULL_ADDER you build gets instantiated eight times to make ADDER_8BIT. That ADDER_8BIT drops into the ALU in Week 6 — not rebuilt, not redesigned, the same circuit. The decoders you build show up in memory addressing and in the control section.

When you finish the CPU at the end of this course, these exact circuits will be inside it.

Two consequences you should act on this week

First: names are an interface. Tab names and pin names are specified exactly in the assignment, and they are specified exactly for a reason. Later weeks look for them. A component whose pins are named differently than expected is not reusable, and you pay that cost in Week 13 — when the CPU will not wire up and the reason is a decision you made in September.

Second: build the small thing once and use it eight times. When you get to the 8-bit adder, you will place eight copies of your own FULL_ADDER subcircuit rather than hand-wiring forty individual gates. Both approaches produce a circuit that adds. Only one produces a circuit you can drop into the ALU in three weeks.

One thing to watch: the circuit names in the video are informal

In the demonstration these circuits get called “half adder,” “full adder,” “8-bit ripple adder” and “two by four decoder” — which is how you say them out loud, not what you name them in your file.

The graded names are the ones on Assignment 3: HALF_ADDER, FULL_ADDER, ADDER_8BIT, DECODER_2TO4, DECODER_3TO8, DECODER_4TO16 — capitals and underscores exactly as written. Three points ride on it, and Week 6 looks for those exact names. The video says as much around 4:02: use the names the assignment gives you.

This Week's Learning Path

Week 3 has two independent tracks, which is a change from Week 2. Pages 2 and 3 do not depend on each other — adders and decoders are different circuits solving different problems, and neither needs the other.

This is deliberate, and you should know it is there. The adder sequence on page 2 is the harder half of the week. If you get stuck on carry propagation, you are not blocked — go do decoders, then come back. Being stuck on one idea should not stop you making progress on an unrelated one.

Six new tabs, same file

Week 3's six circuits are new circuit tabs inside the file you already have. Do not create a new .circ file, and do not delete or replace your Week 2 circuits — they stay where they are.

All circuits for this course live inside that single .circ file. No external file links, no zip archives, no second file. By the end of the course this one file contains a working CPU, and a submission that points at circuits stored elsewhere cannot be graded, because the grader cannot open circuits that are not there.

One habit worth starting now, while there are only eight tabs and not thirty: name each tab exactly as the assignment specifies, the first time. Renaming later is easy; finding out in Week 13 that a subcircuit you have instantiated in four places has the wrong pin names is not.

Where Week 3 Fits

Phase 1 of this course — the first four weeks — builds the digital foundations everything else rests on. Each week answers a question the previous week could not:

Week 1 — Representation
How can data be stored as voltage? Binary, hex, two's complement, floating point, character encoding. Complete.
Week 2 — Mechanism
How does voltage make a decision? Transistors, logic gates, NAND universality, Boolean algebra. Complete.
Week 3 — Computation (you are here)
How do decisions become arithmetic? Adders, decoders, multiplexers, encoders, comparators.
Week 4 — Memory
How does a circuit remember? Latches and flip-flops — built, as it happens, from cross-coupled NAND gates.

The thread is: representation → mechanism → computation → memory. Week 2 gave you gates that decide. This week arranges them into circuits that calculate and choose.
