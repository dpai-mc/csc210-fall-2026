10.13   One byte per instruction

Week 9 traced MIPS, whose instructions are 32 bits wide. Your CPU’s memory holds bytes and its bus carries bytes, so its instructions are one byte each — plus, for three of them, a second byte holding a number. Here is the whole instruction set, the one your final project implements:

Loading the table…

Letters in the byte column are fields: ooo is an ALU opcode, aa and bb are register numbers RA and RB, and caez picks which flags a conditional jump tests. The eight ALU instructions are ADD SHR SHL NOT AND OR XOR for opcodes 000–110, and CMP for 111, which compares RA with RB — the A and E flags record RA > RB and RA = RB — and writes no register. Opcode 111 selects no ALU leg, as you found in Week 7; CMP needs only the A and E flags, which come from the comparator on every opcode.

Two rows are special. END, 11001111, stops the program. It is the same byte as AND R3, R3 — an instruction that would change nothing anyway — so your CPU gives up that one AND and treats the byte as “stop”. And the 0111 instructions move bytes in and out of the CPU; they are Week 12’s subject, and their bit layout arrives then.

Which end is bit 0

Every byte on these pages is written with b7 on the left and b0 on the right, the same convention as your bus loops and Week 8’s address split. Your instructor’s Fall 2025 build pages and videos, which later weeks use, number the bits of an instruction from the left, starting at 0. The bytes are identical; only the labels differ. When a figure says “bit 0 = 1 means an ALU instruction”, it means the leftmost bit, b7 here.

Three instructions need a number the byte has no room for: DATA puts a value in a register, and JMP and the conditional jumps need an address. Each is followed in memory by that number, and the CPU reads both bytes. Every other instruction is complete in one byte — even LD and ST, which take their RAM address from a register (RA) rather than from the instruction.

10.14   Reading a byte

Take 10000110, hex 0x86, and split it where the format says:

1 000 01 10

  • b7 = 1: an ALU instruction.
  • b6–b4 = 000: ADD. These three bits go to your ALU’s opcode pins.
  • b3–b2 = 01: RA is R1. These two bits go to REG_SELECT’s ra.
  • b1–b0 = 10: RB is R2. These go to rb.

0x86 is ADD R1, R2: R2 ← R1 + R2. Nothing has to translate it. Every field is a wire to something you have built: the opcode field drives the ALU, the register fields drive REG_SELECT, and in Week 11 the class bit and the other fields drive the control unit that decides which transfers happen. The instruction is the control-line settings, packed into a byte.

    10.15   A whole program

    Here is a real program for your CPU. Your instructor wrote it for the Fall 2025 class, and it is the program the A tier of the final project must run. It arrives as a file called MYSIM.txt, in the format Logisim uses to load a RAM: a header line, then one byte per line in hex.

    Decoded with the same model as the widget above, here are its 23 bytes. Click any line to see it in the decoder.

    It multiplies 5 by 5 the way you multiply on paper, one bit of the multiplier at a time. R0 holds the multiplier, R1 the multiplicand, R2 the running product, and R3 a single 1 bit that counts the passes. Each pass shifts R0 right: if the bit that falls out (into the carry flag) is 1, R1 is added to R2. Then R1 doubles, ready for the next bit, and R3’s 1 moves up a place. On the eighth pass that 1 falls off the top of R3, the carry flag catches it, and the jump at 0x12 leaves the loop. 5 is 00000101, so passes 1 and 3 add: R2 = 5 + 20 = 25.

    Two details to notice. In the finished CPU, the carry flag feeds the ALU’s carry-in, so the program clears the flags with CLF before the shift of R0, before the add, and before R1 doubles, keeping a stale carry from being shifted or added in. And the whole program is 23 bytes — it fits in the 32 bytes of RAM you built in Week 8, which is exactly why 32 was enough. The last byte, 0xCF at 0x16, is END; building the circuit that recognises it and stops the clock is part of Week 14.

    10.16   The final project, and the plan due this week

    Your final project, due 12/11, is a working Logisim CPU built from everything in your file. It is graded in three tiers by what your finished CPU executes, from RAM, correctly:

    At every tier you submit one .circ file, a test program for each class of instruction you implemented with the results you expect, and written documentation of your design decisions and test results. The tiers exist so that a CPU that runs part of the instruction set correctly is worth something; a CPU that runs all of it is the goal.

    The CPU Project Plan, due Sunday with Assignment 10, asks you to choose the tier you are aiming for, list the instructions that tier needs, write a short test program in bytes for each class — use the decoder above to check them — and lay your work out against Checkpoints 5, 6 and 7. It is graded complete or incomplete, and the point is the feedback: a plan that is too ambitious, or a test that does not test what you think, is cheap to fix in Week 10 and expensive in Week 16.
