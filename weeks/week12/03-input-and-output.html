12.9   Devices need addresses too

A keyboard, a display, a disk or a network card is, from the processor’s side, a few device registers: one that holds data going in or out, and one or more that hold status (“a key has been pressed”, “ready for the next character”) or control (“start”, “interrupt me when done”). The processor reads and writes those registers, and the device does the rest. The only question is how the processor names them, and there are two answers.

12.10   Memory-mapped I/O

In memory-mapped I/O, device registers are given addresses in the ordinary address space. A load from one of those addresses reads the device; a store writes it. No new instructions are needed — the same lw and sw you learned in Week 8 do the job. MIPS works this way. Before each character it reads the transmitter control register and loops until bit 0, ready, is 1. Asking a device “are you ready?” over and over is called polling.



      
Two lines are worth a second look. li $s0, 0xffff0000 loads a 32-bit constant; like la in Week 9, MARS expands it into two real instructions, and you do not need to know which. And the program reaches the device registers with offsets from $s0: 8($s0) is the transmitter control register and 12($s0) the transmitter data register — base plus offset, exactly as for an array.

12.11   Port-mapped I/O, and your CPU’s four I/O instructions


      
The other answer is port-mapped (or isolated) I/O: devices get their own small address space, separate from memory, and the processor has special instructions to reach it. Intel’s x86 processors have both kinds; their in and out instructions are port-mapped.


      
Your CPU’s instruction set is port-mapped. Week 10 showed that the 0111 group of instructions is input/output, and your instructor’s instruction reference names four of them:

The design behind them is a separate I/O bus that every device listens to. A byte on it is either an address or data. To talk to a device, a program first sends its address with OUTA: every device compares that byte with its own address, and the one that matches remembers that it has been selected. After that, OUTD sends data to the selected device and IND reads data from it, until another OUTA selects someone else. Each one moves a single byte between RB and the I/O bus.


      
Your CPU will not get any devices: this course stops at the processor and its memory, and these four instructions are not in any of the project tiers. They are here because they show the port-mapped design in a machine you know completely.

    


    

      
12.12   What polling costs


      
Run the widget with the display busy for 12 instructions, then 0. Every trip round the wait: loop is three instructions that do nothing but ask. A real display is millions of times slower than the processor, so a polling program spends almost all of its time asking. That is fine for a program with nothing else to do, and wasteful for a computer with other work waiting. The fix — letting the device announce that it is ready instead of being asked — is on the next page.
