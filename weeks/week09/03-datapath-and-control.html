Datapath and Control

Sections 9.9 – 9.12  |  Estimated time: ~45 minutes

9.9   Two kinds of hardware

Look again at the figure on page 2. Every block in it — the PC, memory, the IR and MDR, the registers, A and B, the ALU, ALUOut — and every wire between them holds a value or carries one. Together they are the datapath: the part of the processor that values travel through.

What the figure does not show is the thing that made the stepper do something different in each cycle. In cycle 1 the IR took a new value and in cycle 2 it did not. In cycle 4 of a lw memory was read, and in cycle 4 of a sw it was written. Something chose. That something is the control path, or control unit: a circuit that reads the instruction’s opcode and knows which cycle it is in, and from those two things drives a set of control lines into the datapath.

You have built every one of these

PCWrite and IRWrite are each the s line on a register — the same line you raised by hand on REGISTER_8BIT in Week 5. RegWrite becomes the s line of whichever register the instruction names, with a decoder choosing which (Week 10). MemWrite is the s on your RAM in Week 8, and MemRead its e. The dashed “ALU” boxes do the job of the opcode pins on your Week 7 ALU. (MIPS’s ALU has a subtract operation; yours subtracts with NOT and the carry-in, as Week 6 showed. Same request, different wiring.) PCWriteCond is the only new idea: write the PC only if the comparison says so, which is how a branch decides.

The control unit’s whole job is to raise the right s and e lines in the right cycles. That is the job you have been doing with a mouse.

The control unit is itself just a circuit. Its inputs are the opcode bits from the IR and a count of which cycle this is; its outputs are the lines in the table. Week 11 builds one for your machine.

9.10   Why one ALU, and why the IR and MDR

The multi-cycle machine makes two choices that only make sense together.

It reuses one ALU for three jobs. In cycle 1 the ALU computes PC + 4. In cycle 2 it computes a branch target. In cycle 3 it computes the instruction’s actual result or address. One adder can do all three because they happen in different cycles. Your machine will do the same: one ALU, and the control unit deciding what it adds in each step.

It reuses one memory twice in a lw. Cycle 1 reads the instruction from memory; cycle 4 reads the data. Because the second read replaces memory’s output, the instruction has to be copied somewhere first — the IR — or the machine would forget what it was doing halfway through. The loaded data needs somewhere to wait for the next cycle too — the MDR. The IR and MDR are the price of having one memory, and one memory is the von Neumann idea from page 1.

There is another way to build MIPS. A single-cycle design does a whole instruction in one long clock cycle, so nothing can be reused within it: it needs a separate instruction memory and data memory, and separate adders for PC + 4 and the branch target alongside the ALU. Fewer cycles, more hardware, and two memories. Week 11 sets the two designs side by side.

9.11   Same loop, different size

You traced MIPS because it is the processor whose programs you write. The CPU you are building runs the same loop — fetch the word the PC points at, decode it, do it, move on — at a smaller scale and with some different choices:

None of those differences change the loop. They change how many bits move at once and who wires the control.

9.12   The map of Phase 3

Phase 2 gave you the parts. Phase 3 connects them, and this week was the plan:

  • Week 10 — the register file. Four registers your program can name, with decoders choosing which one supplies each ALU operand and which one takes the result. Checkpoint 4.
  • Week 11 — the control unit. The circuit that turns an opcode and a step count into the lines in §9.9’s table; and single-cycle against multi-cycle. Checkpoint 5.
  • Week 12 — memory and the outside world. The memory bus, input and output, and what syscall really does. Quiz 2 covers Weeks 9–12, including this week.
  • Week 13 — the complete datapath. PC, IR, control, ALU and registers wired into one machine that runs its first instructions. Checkpoint 6.
The idea to keep

A computer is a datapath that holds and moves values, a control unit that decides each cycle which values move, and a program counter pointing into a memory full of numbers. Fetch, decode, execute, repeat. Every program you have ever run, in any language, is that loop.
