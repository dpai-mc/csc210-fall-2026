What This Session Is, and What It Is Not

This lab does not walk you through Assignment 5. The three circuits that assignment grades — MEM_8BIT, ENABLE_8BIT, REGISTER_8BIT — are built from the assignment page, on your own time, and the build steps are there rather than here. Doing them twice would waste the one thing this room has that your desk does not.

What we build here is a circuit the lecture course never asks for. BUS_DEMO puts two of your registers on one shared wire and lets you move a byte from one to the other by hand. It is not graded in CSC210, it is not on any checkpoint, and you will not submit it there — and it is the only place in this course where you get to do the thing the wires are actually for, and get it wrong on purpose, with somebody to ask.

That is what this course is. It builds the parts the lecture needs but never assigns, and it is where you find things out by breaking them.

The Question Today Answers

What you have: A register. It holds a byte, it has a write control and a read control, and its output can stop driving entirely.

What you cannot do yet: Get a byte from one register into another. You have never connected two of these to anything, and the moment you do, a question appears that a single register never raises: when does each control go high, and in what order? Get the order wrong and the byte does not arrive — or worse, arrives wrong and looks fine.

What you can do after today: Move a byte across a shared wire, in four steps, in the one order that works — and say from your own observation what happens in the three orders that do not.

What You Will Be Able to Do

  1. Wire two registers to one shared bus and read what the bus carries at any moment.
  2. Move a byte from R1 to R2 using the four-step sequence, driving every control by hand.
  3. Say what goes wrong in each broken order, from having watched it, not from having read it: raising the destination's s too early, and dropping the source's e too soon.
  4. Produce a bus conflict deliberately and describe what Logisim shows when two registers drive one wire.
  5. Write a MIPS program to a specification — not copy one — assemble it, and single-step it.
  6. Account for your own program in hardware terms: why the swap needed a third register, how many bus transfers it costs, and where a “copy” instruction actually lives.

Part 2 — Build BUS_DEMO ~20 minTogether

Two registers. One wire between them. Every control brought out where you can reach it.

  1. Place two REGISTER_8BIT instances from the project pane, side by side. Label them R1 and R2 with a text object so you can tell them apart at a glance.
  2. Add an 8-bit input pin i on the left, wired to R1's i only. This is how a value gets into the demo in the first place.
  3. Wire R1's o and R2's o to the same wire. That wire is the bus. Logisim will let you do this without complaint, and the fact that it does is the whole subject of Part 5.
  4. Run a branch from the bus into R2's i. R2 can only ever take a value from the bus — which is exactly how a register inside a CPU lives.
  5. Add an 8-bit output pin bus on the bus itself, so you can read what it is carrying.
  6. Add four 1-bit input pins along the bottom, 2-state, pull-down, named exactly r1s, r1e, r2s, r2e, and wire each to its register's matching control.
  7. Add two 8-bit output pins, r1reg and r2reg, from each register's reg pin. These are your windows into what each register is actually holding.
This Is What reg Was For

You now have two registers whose o pins spend most of their time driving nothing. Without reg, the only way to find out what R2 is holding would be to put it on the bus — which changes the very thing you are trying to observe, and which you cannot do while R1 is driving.

r1reg and r2reg let you watch both registers without touching the experiment. That is the entire argument for that pin, and it takes about four minutes of wiring before it stops being an abstract claim.

Expect Red Wires and E Until You Write Something

Nothing has been written to either register, so both hold no defined value, and r1reg and r2reg will read EEEEEEEE. The bus will too, once something drives it. Your circuit is not broken. A MEM_1BIT that has never been written displays E, and the first s pulse clears it permanently.

As for why — what is known is what can be observed: a freshly placed cross-coupled pair shows E until it is written. That is a description, not a diagnosis.

Verify before going on — two minutes
  1. All four control pins at 0. bus reads xxxxxxxx — nothing is driving it.
  2. Set i = 10110001, pulse r1s high then low. r1reg shows 10110001. bus is still xxxxxxxx.
  3. Raise r1e. bus becomes 10110001. Drop it again; the bus goes quiet.

If step 1 shows anything other than xxxxxxxx, stop and fix it now. A bus that always carries a value cannot demonstrate anything that follows.

Part 3 — Move the Byte ~10 minTogether

R1 is holding 10110001. R2 is holding nothing. Four steps, in this order and no other:

  1. r1e → 1. R1's byte is now on the bus.
  2. r2s → 1. R2 is transparent and takes what the bus is carrying.
  3. r2s → 0. R2 holds it.
  4. r1e → 0. R1 stops driving.

Do it one step at a time, and read all three outputs after each one — r1reg, bus, r2reg. Write them in the observation sheet as you go. Then say the shape of it out loud, because it is the thing to remember: the s pulse sits entirely inside the e pulse. R1 starts driving before R2 starts listening, and stops driving after R2 has stopped.

Why It Has To Be Nested

MEM_8BIT is transparent the whole time s is high — it does not sample at an instant, it follows. So whatever the bus carries at any moment during R2's s pulse is what R2 ends up holding. Nesting that pulse inside R1's e pulse guarantees the bus is stable for the entire window, and a moment either side.

This is the same move you met in Week 4 with the forbidden state: the hazard is removed by shaping the signals, not by anyone being careful.

Now do it again, faster, with a different byte, and predict each reading before you look. You are trying to get to the point where the sequence feels obvious — because Part 4 is about to make it feel less so.

Part 4 — Break It On Purpose ~8 minYour own, then compare

This is the part of the session that exists because it is not graded anywhere. Nobody breaks a working circuit for points. You are going to break this one three times, and write down what happened.

Reset between each one: drop all four controls, put a fresh byte into R1, and get a different byte into R2 first (transfer one, then change i and reload R1) so you can tell whether R2 actually changed.

A
Raise r2s before r1e

Order: r2s high, then r1e high, then r2s low, then r1e low.

R2 goes transparent while the bus is carrying nothing. Watch r2reg during the whole sequence, not just at the end — the interesting part is in the middle, and if you only read the final value you may conclude wrongly that this order works.

B
Drop r1e before r2s

Order: r1e high, r2s high, r1e low, r2s low.

R2 is still transparent at the moment the bus goes quiet. What does a transparent register do when the thing it is following stops existing? Read r2reg immediately after step 3 and again after step 4.

C
Never drop r2s at all

Order: r1e high, r2s high — and now leave r2s high and change i, then pulse r1s to load a new byte into R1.

R2 is following the bus continuously. Is it storing anything at all, or has it become a wire? This is the failure that looks most like success, because at every moment the value is correct — it is just not held.

Compare With Somebody

Before we move on, say what you got for B out loud and see whether the room agrees. If two of you saw different things, that difference is worth more than either answer on its own — it usually means one circuit has a subtly different wiring, and finding out which is a better use of five minutes than any explanation.

Part 5 — Two Drivers ~7 minTogether

One more, and it is the one the whole tri-state argument was for. Get different bytes into R1 and R2 — transfer one across, then change i and reload R1 — and confirm with r1reg and r2reg that they really differ.

Now raise both r1e and r2e.

Record exactly what you see
  • What does bus read? Write the literal characters, not your interpretation of them.
  • What colour is the bus wire?
  • Does r1reg or r2reg change? Should it?
  • Now drop one of the two enables. What happens, and how fast?

Neither register wins. There is no rule of logic that resolves two devices asserting different values on one wire, so Logisim does not invent one — it reports an error, which is the honest answer. On real hardware this is a short between two output stages, and the outcome is heat rather than a value.

Then the sentence this session has been building toward, and it is worth saying in your own words before you read it: a shared wire needs devices that can be absent from it. An AND gate cannot be absent — it always drives its output. That is why your ENABLE_8BIT has buffers in it and not just AND gates, and it is why the Gates folder contains something that is not a logic gate.

What You Just Did By Hand, Something Has To Do Forever

You drove four control signals in a fixed order to move one byte. A running CPU does that several million times a second, in exactly the same order, without you.

The circuit that does it is called the clock, and it is a small thing — a toggling element and a couple of NAND gates that shape set and enable out of one manual input. We build it in a later lab, and when we do, this is the circuit we will drive with it. Today you were the clock; that is why the four steps are worth having in your hands rather than on a page.

Write a program — the specification, not the code

You have all written code before. What you have not done is written it for a machine whose registers you can point at, and that is the only new thing here. So this is a specification, not a program to copy.

Your Program

Write a MIPS program, in one file named lab05_yourname.asm, that:

  1. Puts two different non-zero values in $t0 and $t1.
  2. Prints both, so you can see what you started with.
  3. Swaps them. After this section $t0 holds what $t1 held, and $t1 holds what $t0 held.
  4. Prints both again, so the swap is visible rather than asserted.
  5. Exits cleanly.

Constraints: no .data section. No branches or jumps — you can encode those this week but what they do is Week 7, so nothing here needs them. Comment every executable line.

It is eight to twelve instructions. The programming is not the hard part and you know it already; what is new is that every line you write moves a value between two of the things you spent the last fifty minutes wiring.

What you need to know to write it

Assembly has no main and does not need one: execution starts at the first instruction in .text and runs downward. Begin the file with .text and write instructions under it.

Stuck on the copy? Open this after you have tried.

Look at $zero again. It reads 0 always, and that is hardware rather than convention.

So: what does add do when one of its two sources is guaranteed to be zero? Write that instruction, run it, and watch the Registers pane.

There is also an instruction called move that appears to do this directly. It works. Question 3 is about why it is not really an instruction at all, so if you reach for it, reach for it knowingly.

  1. Assemble — Run → Assemble, or F3. Typos here are ordinary; read the Messages pane and fix them.
  2. Run — F5. Check the Run I/O pane shows your two values, then the same two in the other order.
  3. Reset — F12, then single-step with F7, one press per instruction. Before each press, predict which row of the Registers pane will change and to what. Then press.

Three questions — answer them in the comments of your .asm

These are the point of the exercise, and the file you submit is where they go. They are not about MIPS; they are about the thing you built this morning.

Q1
Why did the swap need a third register?

Try writing it with only $t0 and $t1. Actually try — assemble it and run it, do not just reason about it.

Then say why it cannot work, in terms of the circuit you built rather than in terms of assembly. What happened in BUS_DEMO the instant r2s went high, to whatever R2 was holding before?

Q2
How many bus transfers is your swap?

Count the register-to-register moves in your finished program — every instruction whose result lands in a register, including the ones that load $a0 before a syscall.

Each of those, in the machine you are building, is a value crossing a shared wire under four control signals in a fixed order. Multiply. Write down both numbers: how many instructions, and how many control-signal steps that is.

Then one sentence on what that number means when a real processor runs billions of instructions a second.

Q3
Where does your copy instruction actually live?

Whatever you used to copy a register — add with $zero, or move — assemble the program and look at the Text Segment pane. MARS shows the real instruction beside the line you wrote.

Copy what it shows into a comment. Then answer: is “copy one register to another” something the hardware can do, something the assembler provides, or both? And what would have to change in your REGISTER_8BIT if $zero were an ordinary register rather than hardwired?

If You Finish Early

Make the swap work without a third register. It is possible with three xor instructions, and it is a genuinely old trick. Get it running, then decide whether it is actually better — count the transfers with Q2's method and compare.

This is not graded and is not on any assignment. It is here because it is the kind of thing worth having tried once.

The Two Halves, Joined

You spent fifty minutes moving a byte between two registers by driving four wires in the right order. You have spent forty watching named registers change one instruction at a time.

They are the same thing. Every line of the program you just wrote selects registers, reads one or two of them onto internal wires, and writes one back — and the selecting is done by decoders, which you built in Lab 3. The wires those values travelled on are the wire you built today. Question 2 is where you put a number on that.

One difference, and only one: what you built is 8 bits; a MIPS register is 32. Thirty-two MEM_1BIT cells instead of eight, still one s line, still one e, same construction and same reason for the tri-state output. A 32-bit register is a wider 8-bit register, not a different device. This course is 8-bit because Logisim Classic 2.7.1 becomes unusably slow at 32, and nothing interesting about a register is invisible at 8.

Two resources worth bookmarking: the MARS repository, and Amell Peralta's MIPS assembly playlist, whose conventions this course follows.

What Comes Next

Today you were the clock. You raised and dropped four signals in a fixed order, and a byte moved because you got the order right.

Everything from here is that, repeated. A CPU executing an instruction is a few dozen of those transfers, in a fixed order, several million times a second — and the reason architecture is hard is not that any one transfer is complicated. It is that something has to decide which transfers, in which order, from a pattern of bits.

Next week is the ALU: the first circuit in this course that changes a value rather than holding or moving one. You start from the 8-bit adder you built in Lab 3, get subtraction out of it by inverting one operand and forcing the carry in — which is where two's complement stops being a formula and becomes a wire — then add the logic operations and use a decoder to select which one reaches the output. And ENABLE_8BIT is what each of its operation legs drives through, for exactly the reason you saw in Part 5.
