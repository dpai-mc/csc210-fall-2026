Why This Lab Exists

What we have: A gate library built from NAND, and the technique that makes the rest of the semester possible — build a circuit once, then instance it wherever you need it.

What we can't do yet: Compute anything a person would call arithmetic. Every circuit in your file so far answers a question about one or two bits.

What we can do after today: Add. Not "simulate addition" — actually add, with a circuit whose answer appears because electricity crossed some gates. And select: turn a binary code into a one-of-N choice, which is how a processor will later pick a register or a memory address.

This is the first lab that does not finish in 90 minutes

Part 1 — The Half Adder ~10 minTogether

The smallest circuit that adds anything. Two bits in, two bits out — because 1 + 1 is 10, and two digits do not fit in one.

1
New circuit tab

Project → Add Circuit, named exactly HALF_ADDER. Capitals and underscore — the lecture assignment checks the name, and Week 6 will look for it.

2
Two inputs, two outputs

Place two input pins labeled a and b, and two output pins labeled sum and carry. Label them now — an unlabeled pin becomes an anonymous stub the moment you instance this circuit somewhere else.

3
Two gates. That is the whole circuit.

An XOR fed by a and b drives sum. An AND fed by the same two inputs drives carry. Branch each input wire so both gates see it.

Look at what that says: the sum column of the addition table is XOR's truth table, and the carry column is AND's. Nobody designed that coincidence — it is what those gates already were.

Checkpoint

Poke all four input combinations. Every one, not a sample — four rows is a complete proof for a two-input circuit.

absumcarry
0000
0110
1010
1101

That last row is the entire reason this circuit is called a half adder. It produces a carry, and it has nowhere to accept one.

Part 2 — The Full Adder ~18 minTogether

Every column of a multi-bit addition except the rightmost has three things arriving: two operand bits and a carry from the column to its right. The half adder has two inputs. So it is not enough — anywhere but the far right.

This is the first circuit you build out of circuits rather than out of gates, and that shift is the point of the exercise.

1
New tab, three inputs

Project → Add Circuit, named FULL_ADDER. Input pins a, b, cin. Output pins sum, cout.

2
Instance two half adders

In the explorer pane, click HALF_ADDER once to select it as a tool, then click twice in the canvas to place two instances.

Notice they are drawn with their gate-level shape rather than as blank rectangles. That is Logisim Classic, and it is precisely why this course does not use Evolution — you can look at a circuit and see what is inside it.

3
Wire the first stage

Feed a and b into the first half adder. Its sum is the partial sum; its carry is the first of two possible carries.

4
Wire the second stage

Feed the partial sum and cin into the second half adder. Its sum is the full adder's sum — wire it to the output pin. Its carry is the second possible carry.

5
The OR gate, and why it is an OR

Feed both carries into an OR gate; its output is cout.

Worth pausing on. A carry out can arise from either stage, and both being true at once is impossible — work through why. So OR is exactly right, and this is also why you cannot simply join the two carry wires together: in a real circuit that is two outputs fighting over one wire, and in Logisim it lights up as an error.

Checkpoint — all eight, systematically

Three inputs means eight combinations. Count in binary as you poke — 000, 001, 010, 011, 100, 101, 110, 111 — so you cannot lose your place and skip one.

cinabsumcout
00000
00110
01010
01101
10010
10101
11001
11111

Do not move on until all eight are right. The next circuit contains four copies of this one, and a bug here becomes four bugs that look like a wiring problem.

It's wrong on some rows — where to look
sum is right, cout is always 0
The OR gate is probably fed from one carry twice instead of from both. Follow each of its two input wires back and confirm they reach different half adders.
sum is wrong only when cin is 1
cin is likely wired into the first half adder instead of the second. The first stage sees only a and b.
Everything is wrong, and it was fine last week
Open HALF_ADDER and re-poke its four rows. A fault in the definition shows up in every instance at once.
A wire is red or blue-grey
Red is a conflict — two outputs driving one wire. Blue-grey is floating — nothing driving it at all. Both are wiring, not logic.

Part 3 — Watching a Carry Travel ~20 minTogether

Now chain them. Each stage handles one column; each stage's cout becomes the next stage's cin, running from the least significant bit upward — exactly the way you were taught to add on paper.

Read this before you build: today's circuit is four bits, your assignment needs eight

What we build together now: a four-stage adder, in a tab called 04-Ripple4-Demo. Four is enough to watch a carry travel and it fits on screen where everyone can see it.

What Assignment 3 requires: ADDER_8BIT — an eight-stage adder, with 8-bit a, b and sum pins. That is a separate circuit tab, and it is worth 15 points.

What changes when you widen it: nothing conceptual. Four more FULL_ADDER instances, four more links in the carry chain, and splitters that break an 8-bit bus into eight wires instead of a 4-bit one into four. The method is identical. It is longer, not harder.

And one thing that does not work — this catches people every year: you cannot build ADDER_8BIT by chaining two copies of today's four-bit adder. It would produce correct sums, and it would still not meet the requirement, because Assignment 3 asks specifically for eight FULL_ADDER instances in one circuit. Build the eight-bit version from full adders directly.

1
New tab, four instances

Project → Add Circuit, named 04-Ripple4-Demo. Place four FULL_ADDER instances in a row. Leave room between them — the carry chain is the thing we want to see, and cramped instances hide it.

2
Wire the carry chain

Stage 0's cout to stage 1's cin. Stage 1's to stage 2's. Stage 2's to stage 3's. Stage 0's cin comes from an input pin; stage 3's cout goes to an output pin.

Give the chain some vertical space so those three wires are visible as a chain rather than lost among the operand wires.

3
Operands and sum

Two 4-bit input pins, a and b, each through a splitter into four individual bits. Bit 0 to stage 0, and so on. Four sum bits back through a splitter into one 4-bit sum output.

4
Probe the carry chain — this is the actual point of Part 3

Place a probe on each of the three carry wires between stages, and one on the final cout. Probes display a wire's value without changing anything.

Now set a = 1111 and b = 0001 and read the probes.

Checkpoint — fill this in as we go

Three input pairs. Watch what the probes do, and write down the carry chain each time.

a + bc out of bit 0c out of bit 1c out of bit 2final coutsum
0011 + 0001
1111 + 0001
1000 + 1000

The middle row is the one to look at. A carry is created at bit 0 and survives every stage to the top — the longest path a signal can take through this circuit, and the reason it is called a ripple-carry adder.

Then compare it with the last row. 1000 + 1000 also sets the final cout, but no carry travels at all — it is created at the top stage and leaves immediately. Two rows, same carry-out, completely different amounts of travelling. Worth noticing that the answer not fitting and the carry having far to go are separate things that happen to share a wire.

What "the carry travels" does and does not mean

Logisim shows you a settled circuit, so it is easy to read the chain as the circuit working through the columns one at a time. It is not doing that.

All four stages are computing at once, continuously. Stage 3 has been producing an output the whole time — it just was not producing the right one until its carry-in arrived and settled. What ripples is not the work; it is the correctness.

That is the difference between a circuit and a program, and it is worth fixing in your head now: a program does one thing at a time, a circuit does everything all the time. Making hardware take turns is a problem you have to solve deliberately — and that is what the clock is for, next week.

Part 4 — The 2×4 Decoder ~15 minTogether

Adders compute. Decoders select — and selection is how a processor picks which register to write, or which memory location to read.

A decoder takes an n-bit code and raises exactly one of its 2n outputs: the one that code names. Every other output stays at 0.

1
New tab and a 2-bit input

Project → Add Circuit, named DECODER_2TO4. One input pin named sel, set to 2 bits wide — one 2-bit pin, not two 1-bit pins. Four 1-bit output pins named out0, out1, out2, out3.

2
Split, then invert

Run sel through a splitter to get its two individual bits. Run each bit through a NOT gate as well, so you have both polarities of both bits available — four signals in total.

This is the step that makes the rest obvious, and it is the step people skip.

3
One AND gate per output

Four 2-input AND gates. Each one is wired to the exact combination that selects it:

  • out0 ← both bits inverted  (sel = 00)
  • out1 ← bit 1 inverted, bit 0 true  (sel = 01)
  • out2 ← bit 1 true, bit 0 inverted  (sel = 10)
  • out3 ← both bits true  (sel = 11)

Each gate is a minterm — one combination and no other. That is the Boolean vocabulary from Week 2 doing real work.

Checkpoint

Poke sel through all four values. Each time, exactly one output should be high and the other three low. If two light up, an AND gate is fed the wrong polarity. If none light up, check that the splitter's bit order is what you think it is.

That property has a name — the output is one-hot — and it is the whole reason decoders are used for selection.

Part 5 — A Multiplexer, and the Difference That Matters ~15 minTogether

We build this one so the distinction is something you have wired rather than something you have read. It takes about five minutes.

1
New tab

Project → Add Circuit, named 05-Mux2to1. Input pins d0, d1 (the data) and sel (1 bit). One output pin, y.

2
Build it from a decoder plus gating

NOT sel to get both polarities. AND d0 with sel'. AND d1 with sel. OR those two results into y.

Look at what you just did: the NOT plus the two ANDs are a one-bit decoder, gating the data. A multiplexer is a decoder with data pushed through it.

Checkpoint

Set d0 = 0, d1 = 1. Toggle sel: y follows d1 when sel = 1 and d0 when sel = 0. Now change the data pins while holding sel fixed and confirm y follows whichever input is selected.

That is the thing to notice: the data went through.

Decoders select. Multiplexers route.

Put the two circuits side by side, because you now have both.

  • A decoder answers "which one?" Its inputs are a code. Its outputs are selection signals. No data passes through it — its outputs go and switch something else on.
  • A multiplexer answers "which value?" Its inputs are data plus a code. One of its data inputs reaches the output.

They are duals: a decoder fans one code out to many lines; a mux funnels many lines down to one. And as you just built, a decoder is a component of a multiplexer — never a kind of one.

The convention for the rest of this course: decoders do control and addressing — which register, which memory row, which ALU operation. Multiplexers do data-path routing, where several sources feed one destination. When you build the CPU, you will use both, for exactly these different jobs.

Part 6 — Finish On Your Own ~45–60 minYour own

Two circuits. Both use methods you have now used in the room, and both are needed again later — the adder becomes the ALU's arithmetic core in Lab 6, and the decoder shows up in RAM addressing in Lab 8. Neither is optional if you want those labs to go smoothly.

ADDER_8BIT — the four-bit adder, widened

  1. New circuit tab named ADDER_8BIT. This is a new tab, not an edit of 04-Ripple4-Demo — keep the four-bit demo intact.
  2. Input pins a and b at 8 bits; cin at 1 bit. Output pin sum at 8 bits; cout at 1 bit.
  3. Place eight FULL_ADDER instances. Not two four-bit adders — eight full adders, in one circuit.
  4. Splitters break a and b into eight bits each; a splitter recombines the eight sum bits into the 8-bit sum.
  5. Chain the carries LSB to MSB, exactly as in the demo. Bit 0 takes cin; bit 7's cout is the circuit's cout.
  6. Set cin's pull behaviour to pull-down so it resets to 0. Otherwise the adder can come up holding a stale carry and be wrong by exactly one on your first test, while every gate is wired correctly. That is a miserable thing to hunt for.
  7. Verify at least: 10000001 + 10000001 (which overflows into cout), one addition with no carry anywhere, and one with cin = 1.

DECODER_3TO8 — the decoder, one input wider

  1. New circuit tab named DECODER_3TO8. Input pin sel at 3 bits; eight 1-bit outputs out0 through out7.
  2. Split sel into three bits; invert each so you have six signals.
  3. Eight 3-input AND gates, one per output, each wired to the combination that selects it. sel = 000 raises out0; sel = 111 raises out7.
  4. Verify all eight values. Exactly one output high, every time.
Assignment 3 also wants a 4×16 decoder, which we did not touch

DECODER_4TO16 is on Assignment 3 and appears nowhere in this lab. It is the same method a third time: four input bits, eight polarities, sixteen 4-input AND gates.

Build it by extending the pattern, not by combining two 3×8 decoders. Cascading is a leftover from when decoders came as fixed-size chips, and it needs an enable input that yours does not have. The lecture assignment says the same thing, and it is checked.

What Comes Next

You can now add, and you can select. Between them, those are most of what a processor does to data.

But look at what every circuit in your file has in common: change the inputs and the old answer is gone. Nothing you have built can hold a value. There is no way to store a result, and therefore no way to do one thing and then another — which is what a program is.

Next week is the pivot point of this course. Two NAND gates, wired back into each other, produce something that is not a logic function at all: a circuit that remembers. Everything after it — registers, RAM, the program counter, the whole idea of a machine that executes one instruction and then the next — is built on that one trick.
