What This Session Is, and What It Is Not

This lab does not build Checkpoint 4. The bus, TMP, BUS1, FLAGS, REG_SELECT and the CPU tab are Assignment 10 Part A, built from the assignment page and the week's four learning pages on your own time. In Checkpoint 4 the ALU’s ci is an input pin you set by hand, and nothing this term asks you to do more with it.

What we build here is where that carry-in really comes from. In the finished CPU, the ALU’s carry-in is not a pin: it is the carry flag from the instruction before, held in one memory cell and gated by the control unit. That one cell and one gate are the difference between an 8-bit machine that can only add bytes and one that can add numbers of any size — and they are the reason MYSIM.txt, the program your A-tier CPU must run, leaves a value in R3 you might not expect.

The Question Today Answers

What you have: An ALU with a carry-in pin, a carry-out pin and live flags, and a FLAGS circuit that holds the carry after the operation is over (page 2 of this week, Around the ALU).

What you cannot do yet: Add two numbers bigger than 255. Your bus, your registers and your ALU are all eight bits wide, and a program that needs 1056 has nowhere to put it.

What you can do after today: Add and shift 16-bit numbers one byte at a time with a carry that crosses between the bytes; explain why that carry has to be copied into a second cell before it is used; and predict, pass by pass, what MYSIM.txt leaves in every register.

What You Will Be Able to Do

  1. Build a test bench around your ALU that feeds each operation the carry left by the one before it.
  2. Add and shift two-byte numbers one byte at a time, and say which byte has to go first.
  3. Break the carry path on purpose two ways and say what each did to the answer.
  4. Trace a real program — four passes of MYSIM.txt — and predict what your finished CPU will show.

Part 1 — File Check, and the Problem on Paper ~12 minTogether

1
Thirty-second file check

Open CSC210_YourName.circ. MEM_1BIT and ALU_8BIT are tabs in this one file. Create today's tab, named exactly:

CARRY_BENCH

A test bench, like BUS_DEMO and LOAD_STORE_BENCH. Keep it: the carry-in circuit inside it is one your finished CPU contains.

2
Add 496 and 560, eight bits at a time

Both numbers fit in sixteen bits. Written in hex, each is two bytes — a high byte and a low byte:

496 = 0x01F0 high 01, low F0 560 = 0x0230 high 02, low 30

On paper, add the low bytes first. You get a byte and a carry. Then add the high bytes and that carry. Check that the two result bytes, put side by side, are 1056. We will do exactly this in Logisim in Part 3; the question for now is only what the machine has to remember between the two additions.

Part 2 — Build CARRY_BENCH ~22 minTogether

One ALU, and two one-bit memory cells between its carry-out and its carry-in. The first cell, CFLAG, is the carry flag: it captures CO when the flags are set, exactly as one of the four cells in your FLAGS circuit does. The second, CTMP, takes a copy of CFLAG before the next operation starts. The ALU’s carry-in comes from CTMP, through an AND gate that the control unit can use to switch carry-in off.

  1. Place one ALU_8BIT instance. Give it 8-bit input pins a and b, a 3-bit input pin opcode, and an 8-bit output pin c on its result. Put a probe on c set to hex.
  2. Place two MEM_1BIT instances, and label them CFLAG and CTMP with text objects. CFLAG’s In comes from the ALU’s CO; its Set from an input pin flags_s. CTMP’s In comes from CFLAG’s Q; its Set from an input pin tmp_s.
  3. One AND gate: CTMP’s Q and an input pin carry in, the ALU’s CI out.
  4. Output pins co (the ALU’s live CO), cflag, ctmp and ci. Every input pin pull-down.

That is the whole circuit: one ALU instance, two memory cells and one gate. In your instructor’s finished CPU, CFLAG is the C cell of FLAGS, CTMP is pulsed by the same tmp_s line that loads TMP, and carry comes from the control unit. Here you drive all three by hand.

Verify, then clear the carry — two minutes
  1. A fresh bench shows E on cflag and ctmp. Both cells have never been written — correct, as in Week 4.
  2. Clear the carry, the bench’s version of CLF: a = b = 00000000, opcode = 000, carry = 0. Pulse flags_s, then pulse tmp_s. Now co, cflag, ctmp and ci all read 0.

You will clear the carry this way at the start of every two-byte operation. Write the four pin changes in the margin; you will use them a dozen times.

Part 3 — Two Bytes at a Time ~15 minYour own, then compare

Every byte operation follows the same three moves — the order the control unit uses for every ALU instruction:

3
One byte: copy the carry, compute, set the flag
  1. Set a, b and opcode; carry = 1.
  2. Pulse tmp_s: CTMP copies the carry left by the previous byte. ci now shows it.
  3. Read c and co — this is the moment ACC would capture the result — then pulse flags_s: CFLAG keeps this byte’s carry for the next one.

In Week 11 these are the stepper’s steps 4 and 5: tmp_s fires with TMP, flags_s with ACC.

4
Three additions

For each, clear the carry, then do the low bytes, then the high bytes, with opcode = 000 (ADD). Record ci, c and co for every byte, and put the two result bytes together.

  • 0x01F0 + 0x0230 — Part 1’s sum. You know the answer.
  • 0x00FF + 0x0001
  • 0x1234 + 0x0102
Compare With the Room — before step 5

A right shift moves every bit one place toward bit 0. To shift a two-byte number right, which byte do you shift first, the high one or the low one? Everybody answers before anyone builds anything, and we count.

5
Two shifts

Your ALU’s shifts take their shift-in bit from CI and push the departing bit out on CO (Weeks 6 and 7). That is the whole trick.

  • Shift 0x40C0 left (opcode = 010). Clear the carry; shift one byte, then the other. Record each byte’s ci, c and co.
  • Shift 0x0381 right (opcode = 001), in the order the room decided. If your result is not half of 0x0381, rounded down, try the other order.

For a shift, b does nothing; leave it as it is.

Part 4 — Break the Carry Path ~15 minYour own, then compare

Two ways to get the carry wrong. Each is a mistake a working CPU must never make, which is why only a session like this one makes it.

Compare With the Room — before break A

Read break A, then, before anyone rewires anything: CTMP looks redundant — it only copies CFLAG. Will removing it change any answer at all? Yes or no, everybody, and we count.

A
Take out the copy

Rewire the AND gate’s first input to come straight from CFLAG’s Q, bypassing CTMP. Clear the carry (now just: a = b = 0, opcode = 000, pulse flags_s).

Now a single byte: a = 11111111, b = 00000001, opcode = 000, carry = 1. Record c and co. Then raise flags_s, record c while it is high, lower it, and record c again.

Clear the carry again — CFLAG is holding the 1 the first sum left in it — and repeat with a = 00010000, b = 00100000. Then put CTMP back, clear the carry the full way, and run the first byte again, the proper way.

Open after you have recorded break A: where you have seen this before

Page 2 of this week refuses to let ACC be enabled and set in the same transfer, because ACC’s output would feed the ALU, whose result feeds ACC, round a loop while ACC’s s is high. Break A is the same loop, one bit wide: CFLAG’s output feeds the carry-in, which changes the carry-out, which is CFLAG’s input, while flags_s is high. CTMP breaks the loop the way ACC does — by being a second cell, set at a different moment.

B
Forget to clear

With CTMP back in place, make sure CFLAG holds 1: do one ADD of 11111111 + 00000001 the proper way (tmp_s, then flags_s). Now, without clearing the carry, add 00000101 + 00000011 as a single byte, with carry = 1.

Record c. Then open Your CPU’s Instruction Set, section 10.15, and find every CLF in MYSIM.txt. Which instruction comes straight after each one?

Part 5 — Four Passes of MYSIM.txt ~16 minYour own, then compare

MYSIM.txt multiplies 5 by 5. Each pass of its loop shifts R0 right, adds R1 into R2 if the bit that fell out was 1, then doubles R1 and shifts the counter R3 left. Section 10.15 walks through it.

Today you run the two shifts at the bottom of the loop — SHL R1, R1 then SHL R3, R3, addresses 0x10 and 0x11 — on your bench, for passes 5 to 8. Your ALU plays the CPU’s ALU; you play the registers, on paper. One assumption, stated plainly: your CPU feeds the carry into shifts the same way it feeds it into adds, as Part 3’s two-byte shift needed.

6
One pass
  1. Clear the carry — that is the CLF at 0x0F.
  2. SHL R1, R1: a = R1, opcode = 010, carry = 1; pulse tmp_s; read c — the new R1 — and co; pulse flags_s.
  3. SHL R3, R3: the same three moves with a = R3. Read the new R3.
  4. JC 0x16: if cflag is now 1, the loop is over.

At the start of pass 5, R1 = 01010000 and R3 = 00010000. Carry each pass’s new values into the next.

Three questions — answer them on the observation sheet

Q1
Where did R3’s extra bit come from?

Section 10.15 describes R3 as “a single 1 bit that counts the passes”. After pass 6, how many 1 bits does your R3 hold? Name the instruction, and the bit, that put the extra one there.

Q2
Does the program still work?

Does the loop still end after exactly eight passes? Does R3’s extra bit ever reach R2, the product? Say why or why not, from the program’s instructions, not from the fact that the answer is 25.

Q3
What your finished CPU will show

When your A-tier CPU finishes MYSIM.txt, what will R3 hold, in binary? What would someone expect who assumed carry-in is always 0 — and which of the two is wrong? Write the sentence you would put in your project’s test documentation about R3.
