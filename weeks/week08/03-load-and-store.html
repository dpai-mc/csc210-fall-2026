Load and Store

Sections 8.8 – 8.11  |  Estimated time: ~1 hour

You can read this page before your RAM is finished

Everything here builds on Weeks 5–7, not on Checkpoint 3. Only §8.11 refers to the circuit, and it makes sense from the figures on pages 1 and 2 whether or not yours is wired yet.

8.8   The only way in or out

Every MIPS instruction you have written so far reads its operands from registers and writes its result to a register. add, slt, sll, beq — none of them can touch memory, and none of them ever will. MIPS is a load/store architecture: arithmetic happens only between registers, and memory is reached by a small, separate family of instructions whose one job is moving data between the two.

That is a design decision, not a limitation. It keeps every arithmetic instruction the same shape and the same speed, and it means the part of the processor that talks to memory is used by exactly five instructions this week instead of by all of them. When you wire load and store into your own CPU in Week 14, it will be this same separation that keeps the job manageable.

All five are I-type — the format you encoded in Week 5. lw and sw were in your Week 5 reference table as encodings; this week is the first time they mean anything.

Where the data lives

In MARS, everything you declare after .data is placed starting at address 0x10010000, in the order you write it. Open the Data Segment pane in the Execute tab and you are looking at exactly that memory, eight words to a row. Your instructions live somewhere else entirely — the text segment starts at 0x00400000 — and the stack starts near 0x7FFFEFFC, which is where MARS sets $sp before your first instruction runs.

8.9   Base plus offset

A load or store names its address in two parts: a register holding a base address, and a constant offset added to it.

lw $t0, 8($s0) # $t0 ← the word at address ($s0 + 8) sw $t0, -4($sp) # the word at address ($sp - 4) ← $t0

The sum is called the effective address. The offset is the instruction's 16-bit immediate field, so it is sign-extended before the add — which is why -4($sp) works — and it must fit in −32,768 to +32,767. The base register can hold any 32-bit address. Between them they reach anywhere.

MARS accepts three spellings. Use the first one:

You writeMeans
lw $t0, 0($s0)the word at $s0. The form the hardware actually has, and the one to use
lw $t0, ($s0)the same thing, offset 0 assumed
lw $t0, valsthe word at label vals. A pseudo-instruction — the assembler builds the address in $at first, exactly as la does

To get a label's address into a register so you can use it as a base, use la from Week 7:

.data vals: .word 14, -3, 27 .text la $s0, vals # $s0 ← 0x10010000 lw $t0, 0($s0) # 14 lw $t1, 4($s0) # -3 lw $t2, 8($s0) # 27

The offsets go 0, 4, 8 — not 0, 1, 2. MIPS memory is byte-addressed: every address names one byte, and a word is four of them. The second word starts four addresses after the first.

Words must start on a multiple of 4

lw and sw require the effective address to be a multiple of 4 — word-aligned. Ask for a word at 0x10010002 and MARS does not load anything; it stops the program:

Runtime exception at 0x00400008: fetch address not aligned on word boundary 0x10010002

The reason is the memory hardware: a word-wide memory fetches four bytes that start on a word boundary in one access. A word straddling two boundaries would need two accesses and some reassembly, and MIPS chose to refuse rather than pay for that. Byte loads have no such rule — every address is a byte boundary.

8.10   Bytes: lb, lbu, sb, and which end comes first

Loading one byte into a 32-bit register

A byte is 8 bits and a register is 32. Something has to fill the other 24, and there are two sensible choices. You have met this exact pair before: in Week 6 it was the immediate field, sign-extended for addi and zero-extended for andi.

The byte in memorylb — sign-extendslbu — zero-extends
0x41  (01000001)0x00000041 = 650x00000041 = 65
0xC8  (11001000)0xFFFFFFC8 = −560x000000C8 = 200
0x80  (10000000)0xFFFFFF80 = −1280x00000080 = 128

When the byte's top bit is 0 the two agree. When it is 1, sign-extension copies that 1 into all 24 upper bits and the value reads as negative — Week 1's two's complement, widened. Zero-extension fills with 0s and the value reads as unsigned. Neither is right in general: lb is correct for a signed 8-bit number, lbu for a character or any byte you mean as 0–255. Page 4 uses lbu for text, and says why.

sb $t0, 0($s0) is the reverse: it writes the low 8 bits of $t0 into one byte and ignores the rest. There is no signed or unsigned store — the upper bits are simply not written.

Writing byte values in .data

MARS treats .byte values as signed, so .byte 0xC8 draws an assembler warning (“out-of-range for a signed value”). It still stores the right bits, but write it as .byte -56 and the warning goes away.

Which byte of a word is at the lowest address?

A word is four bytes at four consecutive addresses. Which end of the number goes first is a choice, and processors differ. MARS is little-endian: the least significant byte is stored at the lowest address.

w: .word 0x0A0B0C0D # stored at 0x10010000 address 0x10010000 0x10010001 0x10010002 0x10010003 byte 0D 0C 0B 0A

So lbu $t0, 0($s0) with $s0 pointing at w gives 0x0D, and offset 3 gives 0x0A. Check it: MARS's Data Segment pane shows the word as 0x0a0b0c0d because it displays whole words, but a byte load tells you which byte lives where.

You may read that “MIPS is big-endian”

MIPS as an architecture can run either way, and many real MIPS systems were big-endian — so that sentence is common, and some course material states it flatly. MARS is little-endian, and MARS is the MIPS you run. When a page and MARS disagree about which byte comes back, believe MARS.

8.11   What lw does to the circuit you built

Week 7 traced if (a < b) from C down to one wire out of your comparator. This is the same exercise for memory, and it goes through almost every circuit you have built.

C writes x = a[2];. The compiler has the array's base address in $s0, and element 2 is eight bytes in, so it emits:

lw $t0, 8($s0)

Here is what that instruction asks of the hardware, one component at a time:

  1. ALU · Week 7 · opcode 000
    Add the base and the offset. $s0 + 8 is an addition like any other, and it goes through your ALU's ADD leg. A load instruction does arithmetic before it does anything else — the effective address is an ALU result.
  2. MAR · Week 8 · a MEM_8BIT
    Latch it into the MAR. sa goes high and the MAR takes the sum. From here on the address no longer depends on the ALU, which is free to do something else.
  3. Two DECODER_4TO16 · Week 3
    Split it. The high nibble raises one row line; the low nibble raises one column line. Sixteen plus sixteen lines, two of them high.
  4. RAM_BYTE · Week 8
    One AND gate goes high. Of all the bytes, exactly one sits where the lit row crosses the lit column. Its h AND v is 1; every other byte's is 0.
  5. REGISTER_8BIT · Week 5
    Read. e rises. Only the selected byte's enable passes it, so only that byte drives o — and some register in the CPU raises its s to capture what is there.
a[2] is an AND gate

An array index in C becomes an offset in assembly, becomes a sum out of your adder, becomes a row and a column out of your two decoders, becomes one AND gate in one byte going high. There is no step in that chain you have not built.

sw is the Week 5 transfer, pointed the other way

A store puts the address in the MAR the same way. Then it has to move a value from a register into the selected byte, and that is the four-step transfer from Week 5 §5.3 with the RAM as the destination:

  1. the source register's e goes high — its value is on the bus;
  2. the RAM's s goes high — the selected byte follows the bus;
  3. the RAM's s goes low — the byte holds the value;
  4. the source register's e goes low.

The write pulse is nested inside the read pulse, for the same reason it was in Week 5: the byte is a latch, transparent while s is high, so the value on its input must not move until s is low again.

Where the course machine and MIPS part company

Two honest differences, and they are differences of size, not of kind:

  • Addresses. MIPS addresses are 32 bits. Your MAR is 8 bits, so your machine can name 256 bytes — and has 32 of them wired.
  • How much moves. lw moves four bytes at once. Your RAM moves one byte per access, because your whole machine is 8 bits wide. The instruction your CPU will actually have is closest to lbu: one byte in, nothing to extend.

Neither changes the chain above. A 32-bit machine has a wider adder, a wider MAR, bigger decoders and four bytes enabled at once, but the path from an offset to a selected cell is the one you just followed.
