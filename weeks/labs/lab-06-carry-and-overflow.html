What This Session Is, and What It Is Not

This lab does not build the ALU. The nine circuits of Checkpoint 2a — the operation legs, the comparator and the zero function — are Assignment 6 Part A, built from the assignment page on your own time. The build steps and test values are there, not here. Walking through them twice would waste the one thing this room has that your desk does not.

What we build here is an instrument. ARITH_BENCH puts two of your adders side by side — one adding, one subtracting — and adds two small detectors that watch their results. It is not graded in CSC210, it is not part of your CPU, and nothing later instantiates it. It exists so you can feed the same pair of bytes to the machine six times and see for yourself when its answer can be trusted.

Last week's page said this is where you would get subtraction out of your adder by inverting one operand and forcing the carry in. That promise is kept — on a bench, where you get to watch it, rather than as one of the circuits you are graded on.

The Question Today Answers

What you have: An 8-bit adder from Week 3, and this week a way to make it subtract. Put two bytes in, get a byte out.

What you cannot do yet: Say whether that byte is right. The adder has no idea whether you meant 11001000 as 200 or as −56, and it gives you the same eight bits either way. Sometimes those bits are the correct answer for one reading and nonsense for the other. The learning page tells you this happens. Today you find out when, from your own measurements.

What you can do after today: Look at an addition or a subtraction and say, from two signals, whether the unsigned reading of the result can be trusted and whether the signed reading can — and then watch a real MIPS processor act on one of those two signals and ignore the other.

What You Will Be Able to Do

  1. Build a test bench that computes A + B and A − B from the same two inputs at the same time, using circuits you already own.
  2. Build a signed-overflow detector from gates, from a rule stated in words.
  3. Read one result two ways — unsigned and signed — and say for each reading whether it is correct.
  4. Find the rule yourself that connects the carry-out and the overflow signal to which reading can be trusted, including the case that surprises almost everyone: what carry-out means on a subtraction.
  5. Break the detector on purpose and find which inputs it lies about.
  6. Write a MIPS program to a specification that makes MARS stop, and explain from your bench which of your two signals made it stop.

Part 2 — Build ARITH_BENCH ~20 minTogether

Two bytes in. Their sum and their difference out, both at once, each with two signals beside it that say something about whether it can be trusted.

  1. Add two 8-bit input pins, a and b, on the left.
  2. Place two ADDER_8BIT instances from the project pane, one above the other. Label them with text objects — ADD and SUB — so you can tell them apart at a glance.
  3. The upper adder: a into its a, b into its b. Put a Wiring → Constant set to 0 on its cin — explicit, rather than trusting whatever pull behaviour your Week 3 pin happens to have, and it makes the two adders visibly differ in exactly one place. Bring its outputs to an 8-bit output pin sum and a 1-bit output pin sc.
  4. Place one NOT_8BIT instance. Feed it from b.
  5. The lower adder: a into its a, the NOT_8BIT output into its b, and a Wiring → Constant set to 1 into its cin. Bring its outputs to an 8-bit output pin diff and a 1-bit output pin dc.
  6. On each of sum and diff, add two probes: set one probe's Radix attribute to unsigned decimal and the other's to signed decimal. The same eight wires now tell you two numbers at once, and that is the whole subject of today.
Why the Lower Adder Subtracts

The learning page explains why inverting b and adding one produces a − b, and the assignment asks you to explain it in your own words. Neither of those is today's job. Today you use it and watch what it does to the carry, which is something neither of them covers.

The detector — you design this one

The carry-out is a pin on your adder, so the unsigned signal is free. The signed signal is not a pin anywhere. It is hiding in three bits you already have, and the rule for reading it is one you met on the learning page:

Signed overflow has happened when the two numbers being added have the same sign, and the result has the opposite sign.

In an 8-bit two's-complement byte, the sign is bit 7. So the detector's inputs are three wires: bit 7 of the adder's first input, bit 7 of its second input, and bit 7 of its result. Its output is one wire.

3
Turn the sentence into gates

“Same sign” is a comparison of two bits. “Opposite sign” is another. “When … and …” joins them. Everything you need is in the Gates folder, and it is three gates. Work it out on paper first, then build it.

Use splitters (Wiring folder, as in Lab 2) to reach bit 7 of each 8-bit wire. Bring the result to a 1-bit output pin named sv for the upper adder.

Stuck on the three gates? Open this after you have tried.

Two bits are the same exactly when a gate you have used since Week 2 outputs 0 for them — and there is a gate in the Gates folder that outputs 1 in that case instead. Two bits are different exactly when that first gate outputs 1. Then one more gate says “both of those.”

4
Build the second detector for the lower adder

Same three gates, output pin dv. Now the question that matters: what are the two numbers the lower adder is adding? Not a and b. Look at what is actually wired into its b input, and take your bit 7 from there.

Part 4 comes back to this, on purpose.

Verify before going on — two minutes
  1. a = 00000101, b = 00000011. sum reads 8 on both probes, diff reads 2 on both. sv and dv are both 0.
  2. Swap them: a = 00000011, b = 00000101. diff should read −2 on the signed probe. Read the unsigned probe too, and do not worry yet about what it says.

If diff is off by exactly one in step 1, the constant is not reaching the lower adder's cin.

Part 3 — Six Pairs, Two Readings Each ~12 minYour own, then compare

For each row of the observation sheet, set a and b and record what the bench shows — the result's bits, both probe readings, and the two signals. Then fill in the last two columns yourself, with arithmetic you do in your head: is the unsigned reading the right answer to the unsigned question? Is the signed reading the right answer to the signed question?

The inputs are given with both of their readings so you do not have to convert them. The results you must read off your own circuit. Do the addition table first, then the subtraction table.

Do not look for the pattern until both tables are full. Then look.

5
Find the rule

With both tables filled, finish these three sentences on the observation sheet, in your own words:

  • On an addition, the unsigned reading is correct exactly when sc is …
  • On a subtraction, the unsigned reading is correct exactly when dc is …
  • On either, the signed reading is correct exactly when the detector is …

The second sentence is the one that surprises people. If it surprises you, check a row before you trust it: does it hold for every row in your table?

Written your three sentences? Open this to check the second one.

On a subtraction, carry-out 1 means the unsigned result is correct, and carry-out 0 means it is not — the opposite sense from addition. Hardware designers call the missing carry a borrow: dc = 0 says “b was bigger than a, and the unsigned answer went below zero and wrapped.”

Nothing about the adder changed. It added a to the inverse of b, plus one, exactly as it always adds. The carry means something different because of what you fed it, not because of anything it did — which is the whole of today in one sentence.

Part 4 — Break It On Purpose ~8 minYour own, then compare

Nobody breaks a working circuit for points, which is exactly why it only happens if there is a session for it. Two breaks, each undone before the next.

A
Forget the carry-in

Change the lower adder's constant from 1 to 0. Run row 2 of the subtraction table again — a number minus itself.

Record what diff reads on both probes, and how far from the right answer it is. Try one more row of your choice. Is it always wrong by the same amount? Then put the constant back to 1.

This is the single most common subtraction bug in a hand-built ALU, and it is hard to spot because the answers look almost right.

B
Wire the detector to the wrong input

Move the wire that feeds the lower detector's second input so that it takes bit 7 of b itself — the pin — rather than bit 7 of what the adder actually sees.

Now run all six subtraction rows again, recording only dv. Which rows does the broken detector lie about? Count them. Then put the wire back.

The detector is three gates and the change is one wire, and whether it is right depends on a question you could not answer by looking at the gates: what is this circuit actually adding?

Compare With the Room

Before anyone looks at a probe: is 00101100 the right answer to 11001000 + 01100100? That is row 3. Everybody answers yes or no, out loud or in the chat, and we count.

If the room splits, nobody is wrong. The bits cannot settle the question, because they do not know which of their two readings you meant. The adder produced one answer; it is your program that decides which question it was answering.

Part 5 — MARS: Make the Processor Stop ~40 minTogether, then your own

The session turns ninety degrees. Your bench is 8 bits wide and a MIPS register is 32, but the adder is the same idea stretched: 32 full adders in a chain, with bit 31 as the sign bit instead of bit 7. So a 32-bit machine faces exactly the question your bench just answered — can this result be trusted? — and it has to decide what to do about the answer.

You are about to find out what MIPS decides, by writing a program that asks it.

Write a program — the specification, not the code

Your Program

Write a MIPS program, in one file named lab06_yourname.asm, that runs three experiments in order and prints each result on its own line:

  1. Experiment 1. Put the largest positive 32-bit two's-complement value in a register, then add 1 to it with addi.
  2. Experiment 2. Put the value whose 32 bits are all ones in a register without using .data, then add 1 to it with addi.
  3. Experiment 3. Put the most negative 32-bit two's-complement value in a register, then subtract it from zero with sub.
  4. Exit cleanly.

Constraints: the two values in Experiments 1 and 3 go in .data, written in hex — work them out yourself, the same way you worked out the 8-bit ones in Week 1. .data first, then .text, no main: label and no .globl main. Only this week's instructions plus lw and syscall: no li with a large constant, no lui, no shifts, no branches. Comment every executable line.

It is fifteen to twenty instructions. The only part that needs thought is Experiment 2 — and everything you need for it is an instruction on this week's list, and a register that always reads zero.

Three questions — answer them in the comments of your .asm

These are the point of the exercise. They are about the bench, not about MIPS.

Q1
Which signal does MIPS act on?

Imagine ARITH_BENCH stretched to 32 bits, with its detectors reading bit 31. For each of your three experiments, write the carry-out and the detector output it would show — remember which adder an addi is and which a sub is — and whether MARS stopped.

Then one sentence: which of the two signals does MIPS act on, and which does it ignore?

Q2
Why ignore the carry?

Experiment 2 carried out of bit 31 and MARS said nothing. From your addition table, pick a row where the carry-out is 1 and the detector is 0. Say what the signed calculation in that row was, and whether its answer was right.

Then say what would happen to that calculation on a processor that stopped every time the carry-out was 1.

Q3
The answer that does not exist

Experiment 3 asked for the negative of the most negative number. Find the row in your subtraction table that is the 8-bit version of that question, and write what your bench gave for it.

Then explain, using the range of an 8-bit signed byte, why there is no correct 8-bit answer — and why the bits your bench produced are the same bits you started with.

The Two Halves, Joined

This morning you built a detector that your CPU does not have, as a measuring instrument. This afternoon you found the same detector inside a real processor, sitting on bit 31, wired to something that stops the program.

The adder in MIPS cannot tell signed from unsigned either. Nobody has ever built one that can, because the bits do not carry that information. What the designers did instead was decide, per instruction, which reading the programmer meant — and put a detector behind the ones that mean “signed.” The instruction is where the question gets asked, because the wires cannot ask it.

Your own CPU makes a smaller choice: its ALU reports the carry-out and does not check for signed overflow at all. That is a legitimate design for a machine this size, and now you know what it gives up.

Two resources worth bookmarking: the MARS repository, and Amell Peralta's MIPS assembly playlist, whose conventions this course follows.
