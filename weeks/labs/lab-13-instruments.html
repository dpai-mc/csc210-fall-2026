What This Session Is, and What It Is Not

This lab does not wire your CPU’s fetch. Putting the program counter, the instruction register and the RAM on your bus is Checkpoint 6, built from this week’s pages. Nothing here repeats its steps.

From this week on, the structured part of lab is shorter — about forty minutes — and the rest of the session is yours, to work on your CPU with your instructor in the room. Today’s forty minutes build two small instruments that make that work faster: a watchdog that lights the instant two parts drive your bus at once, and a step counter that turns the stepper’s seven lines into a number you can read at a glance. Neither is graded by CSC210, and both stay useful until the day you submit your final project.

The Question Today Answers

What you have: A CPU that runs instructions from a stepper and a control table (Week 11), and this week’s pages on fetch and on tracing faults (Fetch, When It Doesn’t Work).

What you cannot do yet: See a bus conflict the moment it happens, or tell which step your CPU is on without decoding seven probe bits in your head.

What you can do after today: Design a two-or-more detector and a one-hot-to-binary encoder from gates, put both on your CPU, and spend the rest of the session moving your project forward.

What You Will Be Able to Do

  1. Design a “two or more” detector for eight lines, and say why it is shaped like a ripple-carry adder.
  2. Design an encoder — the decoder run backwards — that turns a one-hot step into a step number.
  3. Use both to watch your own CPU, and plan your next project step.
CS301 Reference

Encoders and decoders are covered in Saylor CS301’s units on combinational logic. Log into your CS301 course page and open them as supporting reading.

Part 2 — Design BUS_WATCH ~20 minTogether

Your bus has one rule: exactly one driver at a time (Week 10). Break it and Logisim shows E on the bus — but only on the bus, and only if you are looking at it at that instant. A watchdog looks every instant for you.

Every part that can drive your bus has an enable line, and all of them are visible on your CPU tab: the four register enables coming out of REG_SELECT, ACC’s, the test port’s and, from Checkpoint 6, the PC’s and the RAM’s. BUS_WATCH takes those eight lines and answers two questions.

idle first — one gate

“None of eight is 1” is a single gate with eight inputs. Logisim gates take up to 32 inputs: set the gate’s Number of Inputs attribute. Which gate?

3
conflict — your design

“Two or more of eight” is harder. There are two honest designs, and you may build either.

  • Every pair. Two or more lines are high exactly when some pair of lines is high. How many pairs are there among eight lines, and what goes after the ANDs?
  • A chain. Walk the lines one at a time, keeping two running facts: at least one so far and at least two so far. Work out how each fact changes when the next line arrives. Then count your gates and compare with the pair design.
Hint, after you have tried: the chain stage

With x the next line, one and two the running facts: two’ = two OR (one AND x), and one’ = one OR x. The first stage starts with one = x and two = 0. It is the same shape as the carry in your Week 3 ripple-carry adder: a fact that is passed down the line and updated at every position.

Hint, after you have tried: counting

Eight lines make 8 × 7 / 2 = 28 pairs: 28 two-input ANDs and one OR with 28 inputs. The chain needs 7 ANDs and 12 ORs, 19 gates, and grows in step with the number of lines rather than with its square.

Part 3 — Test It, and Build STEP_NUM ~12 minTogether

4
Eight patterns

Poke the patterns in the observation sheet’s Part 3 table into BUS_WATCH and record idle and conflict for each. Before you poke the last row, take the room vote below.

Room vote: in a correctly built CPU, running a correct program, at which steps does conflict light? Write your answer on the sheet before anyone says theirs.

STEP_NUM is the reverse of a decoder. Your DECODER_3TO8 (Week 3) turns a 3-bit number into one line out of eight; an encoder turns one line out of seven back into a number.

PinWidthDirectionMeaning
steps7inthe control unit’s steps output, step1 as bit 0
n3outthe step number, 1 to 7 — put a probe on it in decimal
5
Three gates

Split steps into seven lines. Bit 0 of n is 1 for steps 1, 3, 5 and 7. Write the rule for bits 1 and 2 yourself, then build it: one gate per output bit. Which gate, and why is it safe here when it was not safe on a bus?

Test it with the four inputs in the observation sheet. Then answer Q1 there.

Hint, after you have tried

n1 = step2 OR step3 OR step6 OR step7; n2 = step4 OR step5 OR step6 OR step7. An OR is safe because the stepper guarantees exactly one step line is high — exclusivity by structure, as in your ALU’s output stage (Week 6), not by buffers, as on the bus.

Part 4 — Put Them to Work ~5 minTogether

6
On your CPU tab

If your CPU tab has a control unit, place one STEP_NUM on its steps output and one BUS_WATCH on its enable lines. Tie any input your CPU does not have yet — pc_e and ram_e before Checkpoint 6 — to a constant 0. Click clk_in through one instruction and watch the step number count 1 to 6 while conflict stays dark.

Then, with your CPU on step 1 and clk_e = 1, raise data_e. If you have Checkpoint 6’s run pin, raise run first. Record what conflict does — and lower data_e again.

These instruments change nothing that Checkpoint 6 checks. If you would rather not touch your CPU tab before you submit Checkpoint 6, attach them afterwards.
