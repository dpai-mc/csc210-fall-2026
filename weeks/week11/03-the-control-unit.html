From Bits to Lines: the Control Unit

Sections 11.9 – 11.12  |  Estimated time: ~1.75 hours, including two videos

11.9   Enables down the left, sets down the right

You now have a clock that says which quarter and a stepper that says which step. The control unit adds the third thing — which instruction — and from the three of them produces every control line your Week 10 core needs. Its inputs are clk, clk_e and clk_s from the clock, and the 8-bit instruction ir. Its outputs are the eleven control pins you clicked by hand in Checkpoint 4.

Those two columns of AND gates are the whole reason page 1 exists. Whatever the middle of the control unit decides, an enable can only be high while clk_e is, and a set only while clk_s is. Every transfer your CPU ever makes has the shape of §11.2, without the logic in the middle having to know anything about quarters. The middle decides what; the clock decides when.

11.10   Decoding a class

Week 10 showed that every field of an instruction byte is a wire to something you built. The control unit is where the remaining wires go. First it needs a line for each class of instruction, high when ir holds an instruction of that class:

  • ALU. Bit 7. That is all: b7 is the ALU line.
  • Everything else. Bits 6–4 go into the DECODER_3TO8 you built in Week 3, and each of its eight outputs is ANDed with NOT b7, so that an ALU instruction raises none of them.

Build all eight AND gates now and wire only CLF. The other seven lines are ready for Weeks 12 to 14, and building the bank once means the decoding never has to be revisited. This is the course’s rule for control, in action: a decoder selects — each output is a line with one meaning.

One more line: CMP, which is b6 AND b5 AND b4 — opcode 111 — and is only ever used together with the ALU line. A compare runs steps 4 and 5 like any ALU instruction, then must not write a register in step 6. So the CMP line is inverted and ANDed into step 6’s gate.

Each row is gates you can read straight off it. regb_e is one 3-input AND: step4, the ALU line, clk_e. flags_s has two terms, so it is two ANDs feeding an OR, and the OR’s output is ANDed with clk_s. Every line in a hardwired control unit is an OR of AND terms. Page 4 shows that this is not a coincidence of this CPU but what “hardwired” means.

Three more outputs complete the control unit:

  • ra and rb are ir’s bits 3–2 and 1–0, wired straight through to REG_SELECT. They need no gating: REG_SELECT only acts on them when rega_e, regb_e or regb_s is high.
  • opcode is ir’s bits 6–4, each ANDed with (step5 AND the ALU line). At every other moment the ALU sees 000 and adds. That matters twice: CLF, below, needs an addition at step 4; and in Week 13, fetch will use the ALU to add 1 to the program counter at step 1, while ir still holds the previous instruction. Without the gating, a fetch after an XOR would XOR the program counter.

The demo is the same control unit, with four differences from your Checkpoint 5
  • It counts bits from the left. “Bit 0 is 1” in the video is your b7; its bits 1–3 are your b6–b4, its 4–5 your b3–b2, its 6–7 your b1–b0. Same bits in the same order; only the labels differ. 1000 1011 is ADD R2, R3 in both.
  • Its R2 and R3 are hand-set input pins, with their set lines tied to 1. So at step 6 the 9 has nowhere to go (10:40 in the video). Your registers are the Week 10 core on the bus, loaded by hand as Assignment 11 describes, and your step 6 really writes R3 = 00001001.
  • It does not use FLAGS. Yours raises flags_s at step 5 of every ALU instruction, so FLAGS captures C A E Z each time.
  • Its template also drives BUS 1 and the program counter’s enable at step 1 (03:14). That is fetch, in §11.12 below. In your Checkpoint 5, steps 1 to 3 raise nothing.

Everything else matches your table: the three step terms ANDed with the ALU bit, the quarter order enable on, set on, set off, enable off, and the gate he describes at 04:10, which is the CMP detector — a 3-input AND of the opcode bits through a NOT, so step 6 is blocked only for 111.

CLF: clearing the flags with an addition

There is no “clear” input on FLAGS. CLF clears it the same way any instruction sets it — by letting FLAGS capture the ALU’s outputs — at a moment when every one of those outputs is 0. At step 4 of a CLF nothing drives the bus, bus1 makes the ALU’s B input 00000001, and the opcode is 000. If the ALU’s A input reads 00000000, the ALU computes 0 + 1 = 1: no carry, A is not larger than B, A does not equal B, the result is not zero. flags_s captures C A E Z = 0 0 0 0.

“If A reads 00000000” needs one setting. With nothing driving the bus, the ALU’s A input would float, not read zero. So this week you set the pull behaviour of ALU_8BIT’s A pin to pull-down — the same attribute you set on CI in Week 6, a property of the pin, not a component. Nothing is rewired, and while anything drives the bus the pull-down has no effect.

After CMP, read only A and E

CMP captures the flags at step 5 like any ALU instruction. But opcode 111 selects none of the ALU’s seven legs — you saw in Week 7 that its result floats — so the C and Z it captures mean nothing. After a CMP, only A (A is larger) and E (A equals B) are meaningful. When jumps arrive in Week 14, follow a CMP with JA, JE or JAE, never with a jump that tests Z or C.

11.12   Fetch, and the pin that stands in for it

Steps 1 to 3 are empty in this week’s table. They belong to fetch — reading the next instruction out of RAM — which is the same for every instruction, because the control unit cannot know what an instruction is until it has fetched it. The demo takes shortcuts that Week 13 removes
  • The program counter’s set is tied to 1 and its input pin is pull-down, so whenever nothing drives the bus it drops back to 0 (09:39). That is why the demo never counts 0, 1, 2 (11:58). In your CPU the program counter is set only at step 3 of fetch — and, from Week 14, by a jump.
  • The RAM’s input is a hand-set pin of all 1s, written on every set quarter (05:41), so the demo can put an instruction in byte 0 without a program. Your RAM is written only by a store.
  • “Cycle 2” and “cycle 3” in the video mean steps 2 and 3. The pull-down setting he explains at the start is the same pin attribute you set on ALU_8BIT’s A pin this week.

Until then, you are the fetch. The instruction comes from an 8-bit input pin, ir, on your CPU tab. Set ir only while the stepper is on steps 1 to 3 — right after Simulate → Reset, or after the 24th click of an instruction. That is exactly when Week 13’s fetch will write the real instruction register (step 2), so the habit is the right one. Change ir in the middle of steps 4 to 6 and the control unit switches to the new instruction’s lines immediately, part-way through the old one.
