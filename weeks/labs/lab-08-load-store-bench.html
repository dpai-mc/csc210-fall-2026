What This Session Is, and What It Is Not

This lab does not build the RAM. RAM_BYTE, RAM_16X16 and the MAR are Checkpoint 3 — Assignment 8 Part A, built from the assignment page and the week's learning pages on your own time. The wiring, the ten-step verification table and the checklist are there, not here.

What we build here is the thing around the RAM that nothing grades yet. LOAD_STORE_BENCH puts your RAM on a shared wire with one of your registers, and you are the control unit: you raise and lower every signal that a lw or sw would raise and lower, in order, by hand. Then you get the order wrong on purpose, three ways. In MARS, you take one 32-bit word apart into its four bytes and read each of them two ways.

The Question Today Answers

What you have: A RAM that stores a byte at an address, and a register that can put a byte on a wire and take one off it. Section 8.11 of Load and Store says that a lw is an address into the MAR, a read enable, and a register capturing what comes out.

What you cannot do yet: Make a byte actually travel between the two. Your RAM's verification table drives its pins directly; nothing has ever put a byte into memory from a register, or taken one out into one. And nothing has shown you what happens when those signals come in the wrong order.

What you can do after today: Write the control sequence for a load and a store yourself, say how many signal changes each one costs, recognise three ways it goes wrong — and say which of the four bytes in a MIPS word your machine would fetch first.

What You Will Be Able to Do

  1. Build a test bench that joins your RAM, one of your registers and an input device on one shared bus.
  2. Act as the control unit for a store, a load, and a memory-to-memory copy, writing each control sequence yourself.
  3. Break the sequence on purpose three ways and say what each one did to memory or to the bus.
  4. Write a MIPS program to a specification that takes one word apart into its bytes, reads each byte signed and unsigned, and finds out which end of the word comes first.
CS301 Reference

Load and store instructions are covered in Saylor CS301 Unit 2: Instructions — Hardware Language, and memory organisation in Unit 6: The Memory Hierarchy. Log into your CS301 course page and open either as supporting reading.

What CS301 does not do is have you drive a load through your own RAM one signal at a time. That part is this session's own.

Part 1 — File Check ~5 minTogether

Open CSC210_YourName.circ. Confirm everything from Labs 1 through 7 is still a tab in this one file, and that REGISTER_8BIT and your RAM are among them.

1
Sixty-second RAM check

Open RAM_16X16. With e at 0, its o must read xxxxxxxx — floating, not 00000000. Raise e at any populated address and o shows a byte (or EEEEEEEE, if that byte has never been written — which is correct). If o reads zeros with e at 0, the RAM's output buffer is missing, and today the RAM and your register will fight over the bus.

LOAD_STORE_BENCH

Part 2 — Build LOAD_STORE_BENCH ~12 minTogether

One shared 8-bit wire. Three things that can drive it: your RAM, one register, and an input device standing in for anything that supplies a value. Two things that listen to it: the RAM and the register.

  1. Draw the bus: one 8-bit wire across the top of the tab, ending at an 8-bit output pin named bus.
  2. Place one RAM_16X16 instance (or your one-byte stand-in — see Before You Arrive). Its i comes from the bus and its o goes onto the bus. Give it input pins address (8 bits) and sa, and 1-bit input pins ms, me and mreset on its s, e and reset.
  3. Place one REGISTER_8BIT instance and label it R1 with a text object. Its i from the bus, its o onto the bus, control pins r1s and r1e, and its reg to an 8-bit output pin named r1reg.
  4. Add an 8-bit input pin named key. It is going onto the bus as well — and before you wire it, answer the question in step 3.
3
The third driver

key is a third device that drives the bus. The RAM and R1 can each stop driving it. What does key need, and from which Logisim folder? Add it, with a 1-bit control pin named ke, so that key reaches the bus only while ke is 1.

Section 8.6 of The RAM Matrix says the RAM's output must be able to let go because it will one day share a wire with registers. This is that day, in miniature.

Verify before going on — two minutes
  1. Every control pin at 0: bus reads xxxxxxxx. Nothing is driving it, and that is correct.
  2. key = 00000000, ke = 1, pulse mreset, ke = 0. Every populated byte in the RAM is now 00000000 — the RAM's broadcast write took its value from the bus.

If bus shows a value in step 1, one of the three drivers has no way of letting go.

Part 3 — Be the Control Unit ~12 minYour own, then compare

You are about to do by hand what the control unit you build in Week 11 will do for every memory instruction. Two rules you already know carry the whole of it:

  • Address first. The MAR is set and released before anything is written.
  • Set nested inside enable — Week 5's rule. Whoever is driving the bus starts before the listener's s rises, and stops after it falls.

Here is one sequence, given in full. You write the rest.

4
Store from the keyboard — given

Put 01000111 into byte 3 (address 00000011):

  1. address = 00000011; pulse sa.
  2. key = 01000111; ke = 1.
  3. Pulse ms.
  4. ke = 0.

Now use the same shape to put 11010010 into byte 20 (address 00010100 — row 1, column 4).

5
Load — you write it

Bring byte 20 into R1. Write the sequence on the observation sheet first, one signal change per line, then run it. r1reg should read 11010010.

This is lbu $t0, 20($zero), if R1 were $t0 and addresses started at zero.

Stuck on the load? Open this after you have tried.

It is the store turned round. The address goes in the same way. Then the RAM is the driver instead of key — so its enable plays the part ke played — and R1 is the listener instead of the RAM, so R1's set plays the part ms played.

6
Copy memory to memory — you write it

Copy byte 3 into byte 30 (address 00011110), using R1 as the only place the byte can rest on the way. Write the sequence first, then run it.

Check it without disturbing R1: set the MAR to byte 30, raise me, read bus, lower me. You should see 01000111, and r1reg should still hold it too.

In MIPS this is two instructions: a load, then a store.

7
Count

Count the signal changes in your load and in your store — every pin that changes value is one, including setting address. Record both counts. You need them for the MARS questions.

Part 4 — Get the Order Wrong On Purpose ~10 minYour own, then compare

Nobody gets the order wrong for points, which is why it only happens if there is a session for it. Three breaks, each recovered from before the next. R1 should still hold 01000111 from Part 3.

Compare With the Room — before break A

Read break A below, then, before anyone runs it: how many bytes of memory will have changed when it is over? Everybody answers with a number, out loud or in the chat, and we count.

A
Move the MAR during a write (needs addresses)

Start storing R1 into byte 30 again: address = 00011110, pulse sa, r1e = 1, ms = 1. Now, with ms still high, set address = 00000100 and pulse sa. Then ms = 0, r1e = 0.

Read bytes 30 and 4 back the way you checked byte 30 in Part 3. Record what each holds, and what byte 4 held before.

B
Leave the RAM talking

Set the MAR to byte 20 and raise me, as if starting a load — and then forget to lower it. Now begin a store from the keyboard: key = 00001111, ke = 1.

Record what bus reads, character for character. Do not pulse ms. Then ke = 0 and me = 0.

Compare it with the two-driver experiment at the end of Lab 5. Which bits of the bus show a value, and why those?

C
Load from memory that is not there (needs addresses)

address = 00110000 — row 3, which your RAM does not populate. Pulse sa, raise me, and record bus. Now pulse r1s anyway, as a load would, and record r1reg. Lower me.

Whatever R1 shows now, it is not 01000111 any more, and it is not a byte that came from memory. Say in one sentence what it is.

A Description, Not a Diagnosis

Breaks B and C ask Logisim to do things no working circuit does — two drivers on one wire, and a register set from a wire nothing is driving. Record what your Logisim shows. If yours differs from the person next to you, check both circuits before you decide one of them is wrong; the tool has its own behaviour here, and the useful finding is that the circuit gave you no trustworthy byte.

Part 5 — MARS: One Word, Four Bytes, Two Readings ~35 minTogether, then your own

Your bench moves one byte per load. A MIPS lw moves four, and lb and lbu move one and then have to decide what to put in the other 24 bits of the register. You are about to take one word apart and watch both happen.

Write a program — the specification, not the code

Your Program

Write a MIPS program, in one file named lab08_yourname.asm, that does the following in order, printing each number on its own line (or, in step 3, two to a line):

  1. Declare one word in .data, labelled quad, whose four bytes — from the lowest address to the highest — are 0x7F, 0x80, 0xF0, 0x12. Write it as a single .word in hex. Work out the order of the hex digits yourself, then check it in the Data Segment pane before going on.
  2. Load the whole word with lw and print it as a decimal integer.
  3. Walk its four bytes in a loop, from the lowest address up. For each one, print what lb gives, a space, what lbu gives, and a newline.
  4. Overwrite one byte: store 0x41 into the byte at the highest of the four addresses with sb. Before you run this step, write in a comment the value you expect the whole word to have now, in hex. Then load the word again and print it.
  5. Load a word from address 0 — lw $t3, 0($zero). Then exit cleanly.

Constraints: .data first, then .text, no main: label and no .globl main. The loop takes its addresses from la plus a counter — do not type four separate loads. Only instructions this course has taught: no mult, div, mfhi, mflo or lui. Comment every executable line.

It is twenty-five to thirty-five instructions. The only part that needs real thought is step 1 — and if you get it wrong, step 3 will tell you.

Three questions — answer them in the comments of your .asm

These are the point of the exercise. They are about the bench, not about MIPS.

Q1
What a lw would cost your machine

Your bench's RAM moves one byte per load, at the number of signal changes you counted in Part 3. How many of your loads would the lw in step 2 take, and how many signal changes in total?

Then, reading your step 3 output: which byte, as a hex value, would your very first load bring back if the word started at the address you gave the MAR?

Q2
Where the other 24 bits come from

Take the byte 0xF0. Say what lb put in the upper 24 bits of the register and where it got them, and what lbu put there.

Your R1 is 8 bits wide and has no upper bits at all. Which of the two instructions is your bench's load most like, and why? What would your machine need that it does not have, to do the other one?

Q3
Memory that is not there, twice

Break C and step 5 both asked a machine for memory it does not have. Say what each machine did: what your bench's bus and R1 showed, and what MARS did.

Then one sentence: if you were debugging a program, which of those two behaviours would you rather have, and why?

If You Finish Early

Add a sixth step: load a word from quad plus 1 — one byte past the start. Record what MARS says, and decide whether it is the same complaint as step 5 or a different one. Then work out, from your bench, why a machine that moves one byte at a time would never need to make that complaint at all.

This is not graded.

The Two Halves, Joined

This morning a load was seven-odd signal changes that you made with your own hands, in an order that mattered, and that broke three different ways when the order was wrong. This afternoon a load was one line of text.

The line of text did not make the signals go away. It made them someone else's job. In Week 11 you build the someone else: a control unit that raises and lowers sa, me and a register's s in exactly the order you just worked out — and in Week 12 the RAM joins your CPU's bus the way it joined this bench.

Two resources worth bookmarking: the MARS repository, and Amell Peralta's MIPS assembly playlist, whose conventions this course follows.
