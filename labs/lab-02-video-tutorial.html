What the Video Covers

The whole lab in one sentence: you have been told the NAND gate is universal, and this week you make that concrete by building the other gates out of nothing but NAND gates, then prove one of De Morgan's laws with a circuit you can poke.

In order, the recording walks through:

  1. Renaming your semester file to CSC210_YourName.circ, and a reminder of what is already in it from Lab 1.
  2. NOT from NAND — built with you, step by step. One NAND gate, with the single input wired to both of its inputs. Verified against the two-row NOT truth table with the poke tool.
  3. AND from NAND — built with you. Two stages: a NAND to compute (A·B)′, then a second NAND wired as an inverter to undo the bubble. Verified across all four input rows.
  4. Subcircuits — the technique the rest of the course depends on. Your finished NAND_NOT is dropped into NAND_AND as a component, and you see that the box on screen is a live instance of the circuit, not a copy of it.
  5. Edit Circuit Appearance — how to redraw a subcircuit so it looks like the gate it implements instead of a plain rectangle. This section is optional polish and is not graded. See the note below before you spend time on it.
  6. OR and XOR from NAND — deliberately not demonstrated. These are yours to derive.
  7. Splitters and tunnels — two wire-management tools. A splitter fans one 8-bit wire out into eight 1-bit wires (or gathers eight into one); a tunnel is a named connection with no line drawn, so two tunnels sharing a name behave as though a wire ran between them.
  8. De Morgan's second law as a circuit — build (A + B)′ and A′ · B′ from the same two inputs, feed both into an XOR, and watch the XOR sit at 0 for every input combination. Then the honest part: a NOT gate gets pulled out on purpose so you can see the detector actually fire.
Why the XOR trick is worth understanding, not just copying

An XOR outputs 0 when its two inputs agree and 1 when they disagree. Point it at the outputs of two circuits that are supposed to be equivalent and it becomes a disagreement detector: if it ever reads 1, the two circuits are not the same. Because both sides are driven by the same A and B, you only have to step through four input combinations to check the entire law.

This pattern — build both sides, compare with XOR, poke every row — is how you will verify your own work for the rest of the semester. It is worth more than the circuit it is checking.

Things I Would Have Said While Circulating

These are the corrections I normally give one desk at a time. Read them before you start; most of them are the difference between twenty minutes and two.

1
Set three-state to No on every pin

Click a pin, find Three-state in the attribute table at the lower left, and set it to No. A three-state pin can sit in a floating "neither" state, which shows up as a blue wire and a circuit that refuses to behave. Everything in this course is a clean 1 or 0. The video does this in passing at the 5:22 mark; do it for every pin you place, in every circuit, all semester.

2
Label every pin

Input pins get A and B; the output pin gets Y. Set the Label attribute on the pin itself — not a floating text box near it. Labels are what make a subcircuit readable when you instance it later, and the rubric asks for them explicitly.

3
Two keystrokes worth knowing

Ctrl+D duplicates whatever is selected, which is the fast way to place a second identical pin or gate. The text tool (the letter A in the toolbar) edits a label in place. The video uses both without stopping to name them.

4
Verify by poking every row, not the one you expect to work

NAND_NOT has two input combinations. Everything else has four. Step through all of them with the poke tool (the hand) and check each against the truth table before you move on. A circuit that is wrong in one row and right in three looks fine until it is buried three levels deep in your CPU in November.

5
Only NAND gates in the four derived circuits

NAND_NOT, NAND_AND, NAND_OR, and 02-XOR-from-NAND must contain no AND, OR, or NOT gates — that is the entire point of the exercise. Instancing your own NAND_NOT subcircuit is fine and encouraged; reaching for Logisim's built-in NOT gate is not.

The XOR check circuit is the exception: the comparison XOR in 03-DeMorgan2-Check is a measuring instrument, not part of what you are proving, so a built-in XOR is fine there.

6
Save often, and keep a spare copy this week

Logisim Classic is stable enough but not bulletproof, and there is no autosave. Ctrl+S after each working circuit. Because I am not available to rescue a corrupted file at short notice this week, copy CSC210_YourName.circ somewhere safe once you have NAND_NOT and NAND_AND working.

Circuit Appearance is optional — do not let it eat your evening

Roughly thirteen minutes of the video (15:24 to 28:40) is spent redrawing subcircuit shapes so they look like real gate symbols. It is a genuinely useful technique and it is why this course uses Logisim Classic rather than Evolution — but none of it is graded, and none of it is required for Lab 2 or Assignment 2.

Watch it if you are curious, or skip from 15:24 to 28:43 and come back to it in a quieter week. The drawing on screen is deliberately rough; the point is the mechanism, not the artwork.

Splitters and tunnels: understand them now, use them later

Nothing in Lab 2 or Assignment 2 requires a splitter or a tunnel. They are demonstrated here because Week 3's adders and Week 8's RAM get wide enough that hand-routing every wire becomes unmanageable, and it is easier to meet these tools on a circuit you already understand than on one you are still figuring out.

Build the two small demos along with the video anyway. Ten minutes now saves an hour in Week 8.

Lab 2 and Assignment 2 Overlap — Do the Work Once

The video mentions this and it is worth being precise about, because students routinely build the same circuit twice.

Four of the five circuits above are also deliverables in Assignment 2 for the lecture course. They are the same circuits, in the same file, with the same names. Build each one once, verify it once, and it counts for both.

What Assignment 2 asks for that this lab does not cover:

  • The written truth tables and Boolean-algebra problems, done by hand.
  • The De Morgan's first law check — (A · B)′ = A′ + B′. Note the difference: this lab builds the second law, (A + B)′ = A′ · B′. Same idea, opposite operators. Once you have built one, the other takes about five minutes, which is exactly why the assignment asks for the one the lab does not do.
  • The Boolean-expression circuit from the assignment's own problem set.
