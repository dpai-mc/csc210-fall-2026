Final Project — Your CPU

100 points  |  Published in Week 15  |  Due Friday 12/11, 11:59 pm  |  One .circ file + one documentation PDF

What you build

Since Week 2 you have built, in one Logisim file, every circuit a CPU needs: gates, adders and decoders, latches and registers, the ALU, the RAM, the bus, the control unit, fetch, and in Week 14 every class of instruction, END and the loader. The final project is that CPU, executing programs from its own RAM, correctly, with tests that show it and documentation that explains it.

  • Logisim 2.7.1, and one .circ file containing every circuit. A file that links to another file is graded as incomplete, because it cannot open on its own on your instructor’s machine.
  • Built from gates, as every checkpoint has been. The only Logisim built-ins allowed are the ones Week 14 named: the Counter inside COUNTER_256, the RAM on CPU_TEST as the external RAM, the Multiplexor at your RAM_16X16’s input, and the Clock; plus pins, probes and splitters.
  • The course instruction set (Week 10). You implement a subset of it, by tier; you do not invent new instructions.

Graded by what your finished CPU executes, from RAM, correctly. These are the tiers from Week 10’s project plan, unchanged.

Aim for the tier you expect to reach. You are not bound by the tier in your Week 10 plan or your Week 14 status: you are graded on the tier your submission demonstrates. A CPU that runs part of the instruction set correctly is worth something; one that runs all of it is the goal.

What you submit

  1. yourname.circ — the one file, saved after your last test.
  2. A documentation PDF with four sections:
    1. Design decisions. At least three choices you made in building your CPU and why. Choices the course made for you count if you say why they work: the single bus, the six steps, decoders for control, the ninth buffer. So do your own: how you laid out a tab, how you debugged something, what you would do differently.
    2. Your instruction set. A table of every instruction you implemented: mnemonic, its byte (or bytes) in binary, and its class. Week 10’s decoder and Week 15’s two-pass assembler will check your encodings.
    3. Test results. One test program per class of instruction in your tier: its bytes, the result you expected written before you ran it, and a screenshot of your CPU at END (or at its final state, for tier C). Week 14’s Tests A, B and C are a good start, and Week 10’s plan had yours. A failed test, honestly reported, is better than a missing one.
    4. What does not work. Anything that fails or that you did not finish, and what you tried. “Nothing” is a fine answer if it is true.

The tier you demonstrate sets the range; your tests and documentation place you within it.

Within the range, up to 10 points come from the documentation and tests: complete sections (a) to (d), a test for each class, expected results written down before the run, and legible screenshots. A .circ file that does not open on its own is graded on its documentation alone.
