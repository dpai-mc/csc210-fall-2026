Why This Lab Exists

What we have: A circuit that remembers one bit. MEM_1BIT — four NAND gates, pins In, Set and Q — holds a value until a Set pulse tells it otherwise. That is memory, and you built it last week.

What we can't do yet: Anything useful with one bit. Real values are bytes, and a byte is eight bits captured at the same moment and read back as one thing — eight cells with eight separate controls is not a byte, it is eight bits sitting near each other. And there is a second problem you have not met: once several of these things exist, they have to hand values to each other over the same wires, and a circuit whose output is always driven cannot share a wire with anything.

What we can do after today: Store, hold and read back a whole byte, with one line that says store this and a second that says now speak. And — the other half of the session — write, assemble and single-step a MIPS program, and see the register you just built appear on screen under a name.

Read This First — This Lab Does Not Finish in This Room

Labs 1 through 4 were all sized to finish in ninety minutes, and they did. This one is not. Three circuits and a MARS program do not fit in one session, and pretending otherwise would just mean rushing the part that matters.

So the plan is explicit: we build all three circuits together, we get a program running together, and you leave with working parts and a short, clear remaining task — typically forty-five minutes to an hour of your own time to finish testing and tidying. That remaining work is also Assignment 5 Part A in the lecture course, so it is not extra; it is the same work, and starting it here with an instructor in the room is the whole advantage of being here.

The practical consequence: do not leave without the three tabs existing in your file, even if they are not yet verified. A half-built circuit you can finish is a much better thing to walk out with than a plan to start one.

What You Will Be Able to Do

By the end of this session you will be able to:

  1. Build MEM_8BIT from eight instances of your own MEM_1BIT, with splitters on the input and output and one s line reaching all eight cells.
  2. Demonstrate that it holds a byte — store a value, release s, change the input, and show that the stored byte does not move.
  3. Build ENABLE_8BIT from eight AND gates and eight controlled buffers on a shared e, and explain why its output reads xxxxxxxx rather than 00000000 when e is 0.
  4. Compose REGISTER_8BIT with all five pins — i, s, e, o and reg — and run the five-step verification sequence on it.
  5. Say what a shared wire needs that an AND gate cannot give it, in terms of what is physically asserted on the wire.
  6. Write, assemble and single-step a MIPS program in MARS, and read the Registers pane while it runs.

Part 2 — MEM_8BIT: Eight Cells, One Decision ~15 minTogether

Last week ended on a sentence, and it is the whole of this circuit:

Eight of these memory cells, wired in parallel with a shared Set line, become a register.

There is no new gate here and no new idea. This is replication, not invention — and if Part 2 feels anticlimactic after Part 4 of last week, you have understood it correctly. What makes eight cells into a byte rather than eight bits is the single s line: one control, one instant, eight cells captured together.

  1. Open the MEM_8BIT tab. Add an 8-bit input pin named i on the left. Set its data bits to 8 in the attribute table.
  2. Place a splitter (Wiring folder) with fan out 8 and bit width in 8. Wire i into its combined end. You now have eight 1-bit wires.
  3. Place eight instances of MEM_1BIT — from the project pane on the left, not from any folder. Drag your own circuit onto the canvas eight times, in a column.
  4. Wire splitter output 0 to the first cell's In, output 1 to the second, and so on down. Keep the order straight; a crossed pair here is a byte that scrambles and is miserable to find later.
  5. Add a 1-bit input pin named s at the bottom. Set it to 2-state with pull-down behaviour. Branch one wire from it to all eight Set pins. One pin, eight destinations — this is the wire that makes it a byte.
  6. Place a second splitter, same settings, and wire the eight Q outputs into its eight separate ends.
  7. Add an 8-bit output pin named o on the right, wired to the second splitter's combined end.
Splitters Are Not a Shortcut

You met splitters in Lab 2 as a tidiness tool. This is their first structural use, and it is worth being clear that an 8-bit wire and eight 1-bit wires are the same eight wires drawn differently. A splitter is not a component that does anything; it is a change of notation. A student hand-wiring eight separate 1-bit input pins has not broken a rule, but has made the next five weeks harder than they need to be.

Verify — transparency
sio
11010101010101010
11111000011110000
0anythingholds

With s high the stored byte follows i — change the input and the output changes with it, exactly as MEM_1BIT did with Set high. That is transparency, and it is the same behaviour eight times over.

Now the test that actually matters

The rows above do not prove this circuit stores anything. Do this instead, slowly:

  1. Set i to 10101010. Raise s to 1. o reads 10101010.
  2. Drop s back to 0. Let go of it entirely.
  3. Now change i to 00000000. Change it again, to 11111111. Poke it several times.
  4. o is still 10101010, the whole time.

This is the row students skip, and it is the only one that distinguishes a memory from a wire. The input is moving and the output is not. Eight times at once, from one decision you made in step 1.

Part 3 — ENABLE_8BIT, and an Output That Reads xxxxxxxx ~12 minTogether

MEM_8BIT has a problem, and it is not a bug: its output is always driven. There is no way to make it quiet. That is fine while it is the only thing on the wire, and it stops being fine the moment a second register is connected to the same line — which is the machine you are building.

So we add a stage between the stored byte and the outside world, with its own control. Last week's vocabulary holds: s is storing, and this new one, e, is speaking.

  1. Open the ENABLE_8BIT tab. Add an 8-bit input pin i on the left and split it into eight 1-bit wires, same as Part 2.
  2. Add a 1-bit input pin e at the bottom, 2-state, pull-down.
  3. Place eight AND gates. Each takes one bit of i and, as its second input, e. Branch e to all eight.
  4. Place eight controlled buffers (Gates folder), one after each AND gate. Wire every buffer's control input to the same e.
  5. Splitter the eight buffer outputs back into an 8-bit output pin o on the right.
  6. Project → Edit Circuit Appearance: a labelled rectangle, i left, o right, e bottom.
xxxxxxxx at e = 0 Is the Right Answer — Do Not “Fix” It

Set e to 0 and look at o. It reads xxxxxxxx, not 00000000. Every year somebody reads those xs as a wiring fault, deletes the buffers to make them go away, and in doing so removes the only thing this circuit exists to do.

An x is not a value. It is Logisim saying nothing is driving this wire. That is different from a 0 in exactly the way that matters: a 0 is asserted, and two devices asserting different things on one wire is a conflict with no defined outcome. The buffers are what let this register be absent from a shared wire rather than present and quiet.

If you want the argument rather than the assertion, it is on A Byte That Holds, and Knows When to Speak, and Assignment 5 Part B question 2 asks you to make it in your own words.

Verify
eio
11100110011001100
10000000000000000
0anythingxxxxxxxx — floating, not zero

Note the middle row: with e high and an input of all zeros, o genuinely reads 00000000 — the register is asserting eight zeros, which is a value. Compare it against the bottom row and the difference is the point of the circuit.

Sixteen Components for Eight Bits, and Eight of Them Look Redundant

If you work through what e controls, you will notice something. With e = 0 the buffers are off, so nothing downstream can see what the AND gates produced. With e = 1 the AND gates pass their inputs through unchanged. Either way the AND stage never determines the output. On paper, the buffers alone would do.

They are built anyway, and Part 4 has the reason. Hold the question for ten minutes rather than deleting anything.

Part 4 — REGISTER_8BIT: Five Pins ~15 minTogether

Two blocks and three wires. This is assembly, not design — Parts 2 and 3 did the work.

  1. Open the REGISTER_8BIT tab. Place one MEM_8BIT and one ENABLE_8BIT from the project pane, left to right.
  2. Add an 8-bit input pin i on the left, into MEM_8BIT's i.
  3. Add 1-bit input pins s and e along the bottom, 2-state, pull-down. s goes to MEM_8BIT's s; e goes to ENABLE_8BIT's e.
  4. Wire MEM_8BIT's o into ENABLE_8BIT's i — and branch that same wire up to a new 8-bit output pin named reg, at the top left.
  5. Place one more controlled buffer, 8 bits wide, on ENABLE_8BIT's output. Wire its control to e as well. This is the ninth buffer.
  6. Wire that buffer to an 8-bit output pin o on the right.
  7. Edit Circuit Appearance: a labelled rectangle with reg at the top left, i left, o right, and s and e along the bottom.
The Ninth Buffer Is Redundant on Paper and Required in Practice

Part 3 left you with a question: the AND stage never determines the output, so why build it? The same reasoning applies to this ninth buffer — the eight inside ENABLE_8BIT have already floated every bit, and another buffer on the same control line has nothing left to do.

Build it anyway, because without it the simulation does not behave. Not a wrong answer — an unreliable one. That is Dave's own finding from building this circuit both ways, and it is a property of Logisim Classic 2.7.1, not of the design.

This is worth thirty seconds out loud, because it is a real engineering lesson and not a course quirk. A simulator is a model of a circuit, and every model is approximate somewhere. Logisim decides what a shared wire carries by examining what everything connected to it is driving, and the details of how it does that — particularly with several buffers meeting at a splitter — are not the physics; they are one program's implementation of the physics. Most of the time you will never notice. Here you would.

The rule it leaves you with: when your analysis and your tool disagree, do not assume either one is simply wrong. Find out which. Here the analysis is right about the electronics and the tool is right about what will actually run, and the circuit that ships has to satisfy both. All nine buffers are graded.

EEEEEEEE on reg Before the First Write Is Normal

Finish the wiring and look at it. The wire out of the memory block is red and reg reads eight Es. E is Logisim's error display, and you will see it the moment you finish wiring. Your circuit is not broken. Do not go looking for the fault.

It comes from MEM_1BIT, all the way down. A MEM_1BIT output shows one of exactly three things — 0, 1 or E — and a cell that has never been written shows E. Eight of them, and reg reads EEEEEEEE.

Toggle s from 0 to 1 and it is gone. The cells take the value on i, the wire turns green, and reg shows the byte from then on. There is nothing to repair and nothing that carries forward.

As for why — what is known is what can be observed: a freshly placed cross-coupled pair displays E until it is written. That is a description, not a diagnosis. Same shape as the ninth buffer: the circuit is right, the tool has its own behaviour, and knowing which is which is the job.

Why reg Exists, and Why It Is 18 Points Rather Than a Nice Touch

Two outputs, and the difference between them is what this whole week is about.

o is the bus port. It says nothing unless e says to. It reaches the shared wire, so it has to be able to be absent from it.

reg is the test point. It is always live, it reaches no bus, and nothing downstream in the CPU consumes it.

Here is why you want it. In Weeks 13 and 14 you will be trying to work out why a value is not arriving where you expect. Without reg, the only way to see what a register holds is to put it on the bus — which changes the thing you are trying to observe. Every register in the finished machine has one. Storing a value and putting it where others can see it are two different acts, and this component has a pin for each.

Label s and e on the Appearance

They are two identical 1-bit pins, side by side on the bottom edge of a block you are going to place a dozen times over the next eight weeks. Getting them the wrong way round is silent — the circuit wires up fine and does something else — and expensive to find.

Worth knowing: a circuit appearance's port positions do not have to match the internal layout, so the appearance is the only thing telling you which stub is which. Put the letters on it.

Verify — the five-step sequence, and each step is a separate check
  1. s = 0, e = 0 → o reads xxxxxxxx. reg reads EEEEEEEE and that is correct — nothing has been written yet.
  2. Set i = 11001100, raise s to 1 → reg shows 11001100 and the red wire turns green. o is still xxxxxxxx.
  3. Drop s to 0, then change i to 00000000 → reg stays 11001100.
  4. Raise e to 1 → o becomes 11001100. reg unchanged.
  5. Drop e to 0 → o returns to xxxxxxxx. reg unchanged.

Step 2 is where reg earns its place. Without it you cannot confirm the write happened without also asserting e — and then you cannot tell whether you are looking at storage or at pass-through.

If it is not behaving — the four failures that account for nearly all of them
  • reg reads EEEEEEEE and will not clear. Your s is not reaching all eight cells inside MEM_8BIT. Open that tab and check the branch — a wire that looks connected at this zoom level often is not. One unwritten cell is one E, and one E in a splitter makes the whole byte read E.
  • o reads 00000000 instead of xxxxxxxx at e = 0. One or more buffers are missing, or their control inputs are wired to a constant rather than to e. Count them: eight inside ENABLE_8BIT, one here, nine total.
  • The stored byte changes when you change i with s at 0. Your s line is picking up a 1 somewhere — check that the pin is 2-state with pull-down and that nothing else is driving that wire.
  • The byte comes back scrambled. A crossed pair on one of the splitters in MEM_8BIT. Store 10000000 and see which bit position lights up; that tells you which two wires swapped.
One Thing This Register Does Not Have

There is no clear, no reset and no clock. MEM_1BIT has no asynchronous clear, deliberately — so to zero a register in this machine, you write zeros into it. Worth saying because the Fall 2025 version of this material had a Clear button, and a student who finds that page will ask.

There is no clock either. Something has to raise and drop s and e in the right order, every time, and that circuit is Week 11. Today, you are the clock.

Part 5 — Two Registers, One Wire ~3 minAt the whiteboard

Three minutes, no building. You have just spent twelve minutes adding sixteen components whose only job is to let a register stop driving, and the reason should be concrete before you leave.

Two registers, one wire. Now put a byte in R1 and ask what happens when both e lines go high.

If “silent” meant driving zero: R1 asserts 11001100 and R2 asserts 00000000. Both are driving. On any bit where they differ, the wire has a 1 and a 0 asserted on it at the same moment, and there is no rule of logic that resolves that — in Logisim it is an error, and in real hardware it is a short between two output stages. Neither register wins. Both are wrong, and one of them may be damaged.

If “silent” means not driving: R2 lets go. Exactly one device is asserting, and the wire carries what R1 put there.

A shared wire needs devices that can be absent from it. An AND gate cannot be absent — it always drives its output. That is the whole argument, and it is why the Gates folder contains something that is not a logic gate.

Why a bus at all, rather than a dedicated path from every register to every other one: that is n² wires, and it stops being buildable at about three registers. One shared set of wires and a rule about who may drive it is what scales — and the rule is enforced by hardware that can let go, not by anyone being careful.

The Order Matters Too

Moving a byte from R1 to R2 across that wire is four steps, and the order is not arbitrary: R1's e high, R2's s high, R2's s low, R1's e low. The s pulse sits strictly inside the e pulse, because MEM_8BIT is transparent for the whole time s is high and the bus has to be stable across that entire window.

A Byte That Holds, and Knows When to Speak has a widget that lets you drive those four signals in the wrong order and watch what breaks. Worth ten minutes when you finish the circuits.

What the Two Columns Should Have Shown You

reg changed once in the whole sequence — at step 2, when s went high — and then never again, including when i changed underneath it at step 3. o changed twice, at steps 4 and 5, and never carried anything at all the rest of the time.

Two pins on one component, moving on two different controls, and neither one is a copy of the other. That is the sentence this whole session has been building toward, and the MARS half is about to give it a name.

Part 6 — MARS: The Register File, and a Program That Runs ~40 minTogether, then your own

Now the session turns ninety degrees. A byte in a register is eight bits with no inherent meaning; something has to decide what it means. That something is an instruction set architecture, and the one we use is MIPS.

Open MARS. You installed it in Lab 1 and have not touched it since.

The register file, in about eight minutes

A MIPS processor has 32 registers. Find the Registers pane on the right of the MARS window — that is all 32 of them, with their numbers, names and current contents. Every row is a register.

NameNumberWhat it is forMay you clobber it?
$zero0Always reads 0. Writes to it are discarded.There is nothing to clobber
$v0–$v12–3Return values — and the syscall service number goes in $v0Yes

$t0–$t98–15, 24–25Temporaries — scratch spaceYes, freely. This is where your working values live.
$s0–$s716–23Saved values — expected to survive a procedure callOnly if you put them back
$sp29Stack pointerNo — not until you know what you are doing to the stack

The Hardware Does Not Enforce Any of That

$t0 and $s0 are registers 8 and 16, and the processor treats them identically. The difference between “temporary” and “saved” is an agreement between programs about who is responsible for preserving what — the same kind of object as the instruction set itself. A contract, held up by everyone obeying it.

$zero is the exception, and it is hardware. Register 0 reads as 0 always, and writing to it does nothing. That earns a whole register because comparing against zero, clearing a register, and copying a value are all common enough that hardwiring one constant pays for itself.

Write a program

File → New, and type this. Comment every executable line — that is the expectation for the rest of the course, and it is in Assignment 5's rubric.

      .text                    # the code goes here
      li    $t0, 25            # put the constant 25 in $t0
      li    $t1, 17            # put the constant 17 in $t1
      add   $t2, $t0, $t1      # $t2 = $t0 + $t1
      move  $a0, $t2           # the value to print goes in $a0
      li    $v0, 1             # service 1 = print integer
      syscall                  # print it
      li    $v0, 10            # service 10 = exit
      syscall                  # hand control back to the system

There is no .data section because this program has no variables. When you do have one — and Assignment 5 Part D's program does — .data comes first and .text second.

There is also no main, and it does not need one. Assembly has no entry-point function: execution starts at the first instruction in .text and runs downward. You will see a .globl main form elsewhere and it is not wrong — it is what linking several files together needs, and it buys nothing for a single file in MARS.

Two of Those Lines Are Not MIPS Instructions

li and move are pseudo-instructions. They are not in any of the three MIPS instruction formats, because they are not real instructions — the assembler expands each one into something that is.

You can see exactly what it substituted. Assemble the program, then look at the Text Segment pane: MARS shows the real instruction alongside the line you wrote. Do look, because li's expansion is this week's two halves cashing out at once — it works because $zero is hardwired, and the result is one of the three formats.

  1. Assemble it — Run → Assemble, or F3. Fix anything the Messages pane complains about. A typo here is an ordinary experience, not a sign of trouble.
  2. Run it — Run → Go, or F5. 42 appears in the Run I/O pane. That is your first MIPS program.
  3. Reset — Run → Reset, or F12. Everything goes back to where it started.
  4. Now single-step it — Run → Step, or F7, one press per instruction. Watch the Registers pane after each press. $t0 becomes 25. Then $t1 becomes 17. Then $t2 becomes 42.
  5. Before each press, predict which row will change and what to. Then press. This is the same discipline as the walker above, and it is how you read a machine rather than watch one.

The two halves meet

You have spent fifty minutes building a circuit that stores a byte and can be asked to speak, and forty watching named registers change one instruction at a time. They are the same thing.

The Registers pane is 32 rows, and each row is the circuit sitting in your .circ file. Not something like it — that construction, built from that kind of cell, with those two controls. When you typed add $t2, $t0, $t1, three registers were selected, two were read onto internal wires, one was written — and the selecting is done by decoders, which you built in Lab 3.

One Difference, and It Is Only One

What you built is 8 bits. That is what this course's CPU uses, what Checkpoint 1 grades, and what every circuit from here to the final project assumes.

A MIPS register is 32 bits. That is what $t0 is, and what every instruction format assumes.

What changes when it is widened: the count, and nothing else. Thirty-two MEM_1BIT cells instead of eight. Still one s line. Still one e. Same construction, same two controls, same reason for the tri-state output. A 32-bit register is a wider 8-bit register. It is not a different device.

And the reason this course is 8-bit: Logisim Classic 2.7.1 becomes unusably slow at 32, and everything interesting about a register is already visible at 8.

What Comes Next

You built a byte today, and the interesting half was not the byte. Eight cells and one wire was twenty minutes. The other thirty went on sixteen components whose only job is to let this register stop driving — and that turned out to need something that is not a logic gate, because a combinational arrangement of gates cannot be silent.

That is the second time this course has hit the edge of what gates alone can do. The first was last week: gates cannot remember, so memory needed feedback. Both are places where the abstraction stops and the physics shows through, and both are worth noticing as such.

Hold onto the sentence that follows, because next week's lab opens with it:

A register holds a value. It cannot change one. Next week you build the circuit that can.

That is Lab 6, and it is the ALU. You start from the 8-bit adder you finished in Lab 3, get subtraction out of it by inverting one operand and forcing the carry in — which is where two's complement stops being a formula and becomes a wire — add the logic operations, and use a decoder to select which one reaches the output. And ENABLE_8BIT, the circuit you built today, is what each of the ALU's operation legs drives through. Then the MARS half: add, sub, and, or, slt, one step at a time, predicting each result before you press.
